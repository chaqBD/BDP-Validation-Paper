# Abstract

Supply chain prediction models are routinely validated by splitting historical records at random, yet supply chain data are neither independent nor stationary: line items share orders, customers return, and operations change through new markets, revised service promises and demand shocks. This paper asks how group dependence and temporal change bias estimated performance, calibration and model rankings for supply chain machine learning, and whether the common practice of comparing validation designs identifies the underlying mechanism or merely describes a difference. We compare five validation designs (random row-level, order-grouped, customer-grouped, chronological, and a secondary new-customer estimand) across three model families and two baselines on two datasets — DataCo (synthetic/semi-synthetic, order-line-item granularity) and Olist (real Brazilian e-commerce transactions, order granularity) — using matched training size, cluster-respecting bootstrap intervals, and pre-registered equivalence tests. We then isolate the mechanisms with fixed-test-set dose-response experiments and a factorial simulation with known ground truth, and we examine reliability over ten rolling-origin forecast windows. Random row-level validation inflates gradient-boosted model AUC by 0.032 (95% CI [0.020, 0.043]) relative to order-grouped validation on DataCo, and the inflation tracks the share of leaked sibling rows almost perfectly (slope = 0.229, r = 0.994, p < 10⁻²¹). On Olist, chronological validation collapses a random forest's AUC from 0.78 to 0.49 — worse than chance — a collapse we trace, via an auxiliary operational model and the independently computed feature-drift checks of a governance gate, to a contracting service-promise window rather than declining operational performance. Model rankings change between validation designs in two of three model families on both datasets. We conclude with a nine-point minimum-evidence protocol for reporting a supply chain model's performance claim. The results show that the choice of validation design is not a procedural footnote: it can determine which model a practitioner selects, and in the worst case it can certify a model that performs below chance once deployed.

**Keywords:** validation leakage; group dependence; concept drift; calibration; supply chain forecasting; model governance

# 1. Introduction

A machine learning model intended for operational use in a supply chain — predicting whether an order will arrive late, whether a supplier will default on a delivery window, whether demand will spike — is only as trustworthy as the procedure used to estimate how well it will perform once deployed. The dominant procedure in both published research and industrial practice is to split historical records at random into a training set and a held-out test set, fit the model on the former, and report its performance on the latter (Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023). This procedure is appropriate when the rows of a dataset are independent and identically distributed and when the process that generated them is stationary. Supply chain data satisfy neither assumption. An order with several line items contributes several rows that share a customer, a shipping promise, a destination and, in the limit, a single ground-truth outcome; a customer who orders in January and again in June contributes two observations that are not independent draws from the population of "future customers"; and the relationship between a model's features and its target shifts whenever a firm enters a new market, revises a delivery promise, or experiences a demand shock unrelated to anything in the training data. Random splitting treats all of this structure as noise. The question this paper asks is not whether that is a problem — the methodological literature on leakage and inappropriate validation has already established that it is (Kaufman et al., 2012; Roberts et al., 2017; Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023) — but how large the resulting bias is in a supply chain setting specifically, whether it is large enough to change which model a practitioner would choose, and whether the usual way of demonstrating the problem (comparing a performance estimate under one validation design against another) is actually evidence of the mechanism or only a description of two different numbers.

That last distinction is easy to understate. A paper that reports "AUC fell from 0.78 under random validation to 0.72 under grouped validation" has shown a difference, but a difference between two designs is consistent with several explanations besides leakage: the two designs may have produced test sets of different intrinsic difficulty by chance, the smaller or differently-composed training set in one design may simply fit a weaker model, or an uncontrolled confound (such as the grouped design happening to push more of the test set into a harder time period) may be responsible. Showing that the difference is attributable to *group overlap specifically* — as opposed to training-set size, test-set composition, or any other feature of the split — requires an experimental design in which group overlap is the only thing that changes while everything else, including the identity of the test set, is held fixed. Few empirical papers in the supply chain machine learning literature run that experiment. Most run the descriptive comparison once, on one model, on one dataset, and treat the resulting gap as self-evidently a leakage effect.

This paper does run that experiment, on two datasets, across three model families, with the training set and test set size held constant and only the share of leaked dependence varied in controlled steps. We also run a complementary experiment in the opposite direction: a fully synthetic simulation in which the magnitude of group dependence and of several forms of temporal change are set by the experimenter, so that the "true" effect of each mechanism on an estimated performance contrast is known exactly, and the question becomes whether the standard descriptive contrasts used in practice actually recover that known truth. Running both experiments on the same study, rather than one or the other, is what lets us separate a methodological framework (quantifying *how much* a validation design biases an estimate, and showing that the bias tracks a specific, manipulable mechanism) from a purely empirical demonstration (showing that two numbers differ).

The empirical setting is deliberately dual. DataCo Smart Supply Chain is a widely used, order-line-item-level dataset whose late-delivery outcome is, as we confirm directly, perfectly determined at the order level (every line item of a given order carries an identical label), making it an unusually clean testbed for isolating group-dependence leakage but a poor testbed for anything that depends on a noisy, realistic outcome-generating process. Olist is a real Brazilian e-commerce marketplace dataset at order granularity, with a genuinely noisy late-delivery outcome and a documented history of operational change — including, as we show, a sharp contraction in the promised delivery window during 2018 — making it the complementary testbed for temporal change. Neither dataset alone would support the paper's claims; together, they let us show that group dependence and temporal change are two distinct mechanisms, that they do not require the same validation fix, and that a model family's exposure to each mechanism differs (a finding that itself undermines the idea of a model-independent "correction" for validation bias).

The paper makes three contributions. First, a methodological framework that separates descriptive validation-design contrasts from mechanisms identified by controlled, fixed-test-set experiments and by simulations with known ground truth, paired with uncertainty methods — a paired cluster bootstrap and a block bootstrap — that respect the dependence structure the paper is about, rather than the independent-replication assumption that a repeated-random-split procedure silently makes. Second, empirical evidence, across two datasets and three model families, of when order dependence, a changing service promise and temporal drift alter performance, calibration and model selection, including a case in which temporal drift is present without any accompanying deterioration in next-period performance — directly consistent with Eck et al.'s (2022) finding that supply chain models can drift without degrading. Third, a reporting protocol — nine concrete requirements, derived from the paper's own results rather than asserted in the abstract — for the minimum evidence a performance claim for a supply chain model should carry before a practitioner relies on it, together with an assessment of what an automated model-governance gate independently catches and what it misses when run naively.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on data leakage, validation design for dependent and non-stationary data, calibration, and machine learning in supply chain management, and identifies the specific gap this paper addresses. Section 3 states the research questions and the hypotheses registered before the final evaluation. Section 4 describes the data, the prediction tasks, the five validation designs, the model families, the fixed-test-set and simulation designs, the rolling-origin analysis, and the statistical methods, including the governance-gate integration. Section 5 reports results organised by objective. Section 6 discusses what the results mean for practice and for the methodological literature, including where the paper's own design is itself open to the same criticisms it levels at others. Section 7 states the limitations plainly. Section 8 concludes.

# 2. Literature review

## 2.1 Leakage and the reproducibility of machine-learning-based findings

The broadest frame for this paper is the growing literature documenting that a substantial share of published machine learning results, particularly in applied domains, do not hold up once validation errors are corrected. Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) surveyed 17 research fields and found data leakage — in one of eight taxonomised forms — implicated in 294 papers; in their own re-analysis of a civil-war prediction application, correcting the leakage eliminated the claimed superiority of machine learning models over decades-old statistical baselines entirely. Kaufman, Rosset, Perlich and Stitelman (2012) had earlier formalised leakage as the introduction of information about the target that would not be legitimately available at the time a prediction must be made, and catalogued its common sources: features derived from the outcome, features computed over a window that includes the future, and — the form most relevant to the present paper — test instances that are not truly independent of the training instances because they share an unobserved common cause or a direct structural link, such as membership in the same group. Neither paper is supply-chain-specific, but both motivate the present paper's starting premise: a performance estimate is only as credible as the independence of the split that produced it, and that independence cannot be assumed, it has to be checked.

## 2.2 Validation design for dependent and non-stationary data

Outside machine learning, the statistical and ecological literatures worked out the consequences of ignoring dependence structure in validation well before it became a prominent concern in applied ML. Roberts et al. (2017) reviewed cross-validation strategies for data with temporal, spatial, hierarchical or phylogenetic structure and showed, across several ecological case studies, that random k-fold cross-validation systematically overestimates predictive performance whenever such structure is present, and that the appropriate fix — blocking the folds along the dependence structure, whether that is geographic distance, time, or a hierarchical grouping variable — depends on which structure is present and on the question the model is meant to answer (interpolation within a known population versus extrapolation to an unseen one). The present paper's order-grouped and customer-grouped designs are direct supply chain analogues of Roberts et al.'s hierarchical blocking.

The time series forecasting literature arrived independently at a parallel conclusion specifically for temporal dependence. Tashman (2000) reviewed decades of out-of-sample forecast evaluation practice and argued that evaluating a forecasting method only within-sample, or on a single arbitrarily chosen holdout, systematically favours methods that overfit the idiosyncrasies of the available history; he advocated what later became standardised as rolling-origin evaluation, in which the model is re-evaluated at successive forecast origins moving forward through time. Bergmeir and Benítez (2012) examined cross-validation specifically for time series predictor evaluation and showed that, contrary to a common intuition that cross-validation is simply inappropriate for time-dependent data, a blocked or modified cross-validation can in some circumstances still be used productively, provided the blocking respects the autocorrelation structure — a nuance the present paper's rolling-origin design (Section 4.6) is built to respect by using only expanding, forward-looking windows.

A smaller methodological literature addresses the uncertainty quantification that should accompany a validation-design comparison once dependence is acknowledged. Künsch (1989) introduced the block bootstrap for general stationary time series, in which contiguous blocks of observations, rather than individual observations, are resampled, preserving within-block dependence under resampling. Field and Welsh (2007) extended cluster-respecting resampling to clustered (rather than purely temporal) dependence, establishing conditions under which resampling whole clusters — orders, in the present paper's case — produces valid confidence intervals when the usual i.i.d. bootstrap would not. Repeated random train/test splits, by contrast, are not independent replications of anything and do not produce a valid confidence interval for a performance estimate under dependence; this paper's design (Section 4.7) uses cluster and block bootstraps throughout and treats this distinction as load-bearing rather than a technical footnote.

## 2.3 Model families, flexibility, and why leakage is not model-agnostic

The present paper's results (Section 5) turn out to depend materially on which model family is used to detect a given mechanism, which makes it necessary to situate the two non-linear model families used throughout — random forests (Breiman, 2001) and gradient-boosted decision trees, here implemented as LightGBM (Ke et al., 2017) — within the leakage literature specifically, rather than treat them only as performance benchmarks. Both are ensembles of decision trees fit to successively finer partitions of the feature space; given sufficient depth, sufficiently many trees, and a sufficiently permissive minimum-leaf-size setting, such an ensemble can, in principle, carve out a partition cell narrow enough to correspond to a single training group, effectively memorising that group's label rather than learning a relationship that generalises to groups the model has not seen. A linearly parameterised model, such as the regularised logistic regression used throughout this paper, cannot do this: its decision boundary is a smooth function of the input space, so a relationship it has learned from one group's covariate-label pair generalises, by construction, to any other point with similar covariates, whether or not that specific group appeared in training. This distinction, which Section 5.4 and Section 5.6 both confirm empirically, is not a minor implementation detail; it means that a leakage audit conducted on one model family cannot be assumed to transfer to another, and that published benchmarks comparing model families under a single, possibly leakage-prone validation design may be comparing not model quality but differential exposure to a leakage mechanism the benchmark was never designed to detect.

## 2.4 Calibration as a distinct failure mode from discrimination

A model can rank cases correctly — achieve high AUC — while every probability it outputs is systematically wrong, because discrimination and calibration are mathematically independent properties of a classifier (Van Calster, McLernon, van Smeden, Wynants, & Steyerberg, 2019). Van Calster et al. argue, in a clinical prediction modelling context directly transferable to operational risk scoring, that calibration failures are underreported relative to discrimination failures because AUC is reported by default in most software while calibration requires deliberate additional analysis, and that this asymmetry in reporting convenience, not in importance, explains calibration's comparative neglect in the applied literature. The present paper's results bear this out directly: one of the paper's sharpest findings — a baseline model's calibration slope of 11.9, indicating severe overconfidence, appearing alongside a seemingly respectable AUC of 0.80 — would have been invisible to a study that reported discrimination alone. The methodological literature on calibration itself has long distinguished a model's native calibration from its *recalibratability*: Platt (1999) showed that a support vector machine's uncalibrated scores could be mapped to well-calibrated probabilities with a simple post-hoc logistic transform, and Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005) showed empirically that different model families are native miscalibrated in systematically different directions — boosted trees tend toward sigmoidal under-confidence near the extremes, while bagged trees such as random forests tend toward the opposite — a finding with a direct bearing on the present paper, since it means a validation-design-induced calibration failure (Section 5.2, 5.8) is superimposed on each model family's own baseline miscalibration tendency rather than starting from a common, neutral baseline, and that recalibration alone, performed without first correcting the validation design that produced the miscalibrated estimate, would correct the wrong problem.

## 2.5 Monitoring deployed models and the drift-without-deterioration finding

Eck, Kabakci-Zorlu, Chen, Savard and Bao (2022) proposed a monitoring framework for deployed machine learning models, with supply chain examples, and documented empirically that feature or prediction drift does not always precede or accompany a decline in realised performance — a model can drift and remain accurate, or remain stable in its inputs while its accuracy degrades for reasons orthogonal to the measured drift signal. This finding is the direct motivation for the present paper's Section 5.7, where we show a DataCo origin with elevated feature drift followed by *improving*, not worsening, next-period AUC, and argue, as Eck et al. do, that a drift alarm and a performance alarm are not interchangeable and should be reported as two separate quantities rather than one proxy for the other. The broader concept-drift monitoring literature (Hinder, Vaquet, & Hammer, 2024a, 2024b) extends this point methodologically, distinguishing the unsupervised detection of a distributional shift from the harder, and practically more consequential, problem of localising and explaining where and why a shift has occurred — a distinction this paper operationalises through its auxiliary operational model (Section 4.6), which separates a shift in the *service promise itself* from a shift in the *firm's operational ability to meet any given promise*.

## 2.6 Machine learning in supply chain management: scale of adoption and scale of validation scrutiny

The volume of published work applying machine learning to supply chain problems has grown sharply over the past decade. Douaioui, Oucheikh, Benmoussa and Mabrouki (2024) reviewed 119 papers on machine learning and deep learning for demand forecasting in supply chain management published between 2015 and 2024, finding a steep rise in publication volume and increasing adoption of ensemble and deep learning architectures over purely statistical baselines. Vlachos and Pulagam (2025) conducted a broader systematic review of machine learning across supply chain management functions, extending into the emergent use of large language models, and catalogued both the themes practitioners and researchers have pursued and the barriers — data quality, interpretability, organisational trust — that have slowed adoption relative to the technical literature's optimism. Neither review, nor the broader literature they summarise, treats validation methodology as a first-class concern in the way the general machine learning methodology literature (Section 2.1–2.2) does; validation design appears, when it appears at all, as a brief methods-section sentence reporting a train/test split ratio, rarely accompanied by a justification for why that split is appropriate to the data's dependence structure. This is the specific gap the present paper addresses: not whether machine learning works for supply chain prediction — the review literature already answers that affirmatively, with qualifications — but whether the field's standard way of measuring how well it works is itself reliable.

## 2.7 Model governance and the limits of automated checks

A parallel literature has developed tooling and process frameworks to catch exactly the failure modes the empirical literature above documents, before a model reaches production. Mitchell et al. (2019) proposed model cards, short structured documents accompanying a released model that report its performance across relevant subgroups and the conditions under which it was evaluated, explicitly to prevent models from being deployed into contexts their evaluation did not cover. Raji et al. (2020) went further, proposing an end-to-end internal algorithmic auditing framework intended to catch problems — including validation-methodology problems — during development rather than after an external audit or an incident discovers them post hoc. The present paper integrates a representative tool from this literature, the BDP Model Gate, directly into its own evaluation pipeline (Section 4.8) and treats its output as a second, independently computed source of evidence rather than as a replacement for the paper's own bootstrap-based hypothesis tests — a design choice that lets us report, concretely, both what the gate catches on its own (including a drift signature this paper's own analysis had not been tracking) and where its generic, deployment-oriented thresholds require deliberate adaptation to a research setting (Section 4.8, Section 6.5).

## 2.8 Positioning

Taken together, the literature establishes that validation leakage is a real and widespread problem (Section 2.1), that the appropriate validation design depends on the specific dependence structure present (Section 2.2), that discrimination and calibration must be assessed separately (Section 2.3), that drift and deterioration are not the same thing (Section 2.4), that supply chain machine learning has grown rapidly without a commensurate growth in validation scrutiny (Section 2.5), and that automated governance tooling exists to catch some, but not all, of these issues (Section 2.6). What remains unestablished — and what this paper supplies — is a controlled, mechanism-identifying account of how large these effects are specifically in a supply chain prediction setting, whether the standard descriptive validation-design comparison is itself reliable evidence of the mechanism it is taken to demonstrate, and whether these effects are large enough, and heterogeneous enough across model families, to change which model a practitioner selects.

# 3. Research questions and hypotheses

Four research questions, each with one or more hypotheses, were registered before the final evaluation on held-out data; margins, tests, and the registration itself are reported transparently rather than asserted after the fact, in line with Lakens's (2017) equivalence-testing framework, which this paper adopts to avoid treating "the confidence interval excludes zero" as equivalent to "the difference is large enough to matter."

**RQ1 — How sensitive are performance estimates to the validation design?** H1 states that on DataCo, row-level random AUC exceeds order-grouped AUC by more than an equivalence margin of δ = 0.01 AUC. H2 states that on Olist, chronological AUC is lower than order-level random AUC by more than δ. Both are tested with a paired cluster-bootstrap interval for the difference, compared against δ, separately for each of three model families, with Holm's (1979) step-down procedure controlling the family-wise error rate across the resulting multiple tests.

**RQ2 — Do design contrasts identify the mechanism, or only describe a difference?** H3 states that in a fixed-test-set experiment on DataCo, AUC increases monotonically with the share of test orders whose sibling line items are present in training. H4 states that in a factorial simulation with known, injected mechanisms, the random-vs-grouped performance contrast tracks the magnitude of injected within-group dependence, the grouped-vs-chronological contrast tracks the magnitude of injected temporal change, and an interaction appears when both are present simultaneously. H3 is tested with a slope test on the dose-response relationship; H4 is tested by comparing the contrast estimated from a modest-sized replicate against the same contrast computed from a very large reference population, treated as the population truth, yielding a recovery error.

**RQ3 — Which changes over time are associated with deterioration?** H5 states that feature drift (measured by the population stability index) is only weakly associated with the next period's change in AUC, whereas a change in lateness prevalence is associated with a change in calibration-in-the-large — the expectation, following Eck et al. (2022), being that drift and deterioration are empirically dissociable. H5 is tested with an origin-level regression with interval estimates, at the rolling-origin level described in Section 4.6.

**RQ4 — Do validation choices change model selection?** H6 states that model rankings under row-level random validation differ from rankings under the paper's primary validation design (chronological, orders intact, returning customers allowed). H6 is tested with Kendall's τ, with a bootstrap interval, and the raw rank-change frequency, computed separately on each dataset.

Hypotheses state expected directions; as the results in Section 5 show plainly, not every hypothesis is confirmed for every model family, and the paper reports the disconfirming cases with the same prominence as the confirming ones.

# 4. Data and methods

## 4.1 Datasets and prediction tasks

**DataCo Smart Supply Chain** (Constante, Silva, & Pereira, 2019) is a widely circulated order-line-item-level dataset covering January 2015 to January 2018, 180,519 rows across 65,752 orders (2.75 line items per order on average) and 20,652 customers. The outcome, `Late_delivery_risk`, is recorded at the order-line-item level but, as our own audit confirms, is identical across every line item of a given order (100.0% of orders have a single label across all their lines) — the outcome is, in effect, an order-level property duplicated onto each of an order's constituent rows. Decision time is order placement. The eligible population excludes the 7,754 rows (4.3%) coded as cancelled, consistent with the source data's convention of recording a cancelled order as "not late," an outcome-availability artefact rather than a genuine on-time delivery. The primary analysis population (non-cancelled, January 2015 to September 2017, to leave a held-out sensitivity window) comprises 164,401 rows, 54,796 orders and 12,296 customers, with a 57.3% late rate. Twenty-three predictors available at order-placement time are retained (twelve numeric, eleven categorical); predictors recorded only after the delivery outcome is known (actual shipping days, delivery status, order status, shipping date) are excluded, as are identifiers, free text and personally identifying fields, which are retained only as grouping keys, never as model inputs. Because the outcome is close to deterministic given order identity, DataCo is an unusually clean instrument for isolating the consequences of group-dependence leakage, and an unusually poor instrument for anything requiring a realistic, noisy outcome-generating process — a limitation we return to in Section 7.

**Olist** (Olist, 2018) is a real Brazilian e-commerce marketplace dataset at order granularity, covering September 2016 to October 2018. The eligible population restricts to delivered orders purchased between January 2017 and August 2018 whose promised delivery date falls at least two weeks before the last recorded delivery date in the data, so that a late outcome, had one occurred, would already be observable rather than right-censored by the data extraction date; eight orders marked "delivered" with no recorded delivery date — a documented data-quality artefact in this dataset — are excluded from the eligible population on the same logic used for DataCo's cancelled rows, rather than coded as either on-time or late. The resulting primary population comprises 96,180 orders and 93,078 distinct customers, with an 8.1% late rate (monthly rates in the primary window range from 1.4% to 21.4%, confirming substantial temporal heterogeneity even before any formal drift analysis). The outcome is delivery after the promised date. Seventeen predictors (fourteen numeric, three categorical) available at purchase time are retained, including an aggregation of order items, payments and products to the order level, a count of each customer's prior orders (a legitimate feature, since it reflects history available before the decision), and the promised delivery window itself; approval, carrier and delivery timestamps and review data, all recorded after the decision point, are excluded.

## 4.2 The five validation designs

Five validation designs are compared, following the estimand table registered in the analysis plan. **Row-level random** splits at the individual row with no regard for order, customer or time — the common practice in published applied work, and the paper's explicit baseline for "what is typically reported." **Order-grouped random** splits at the order level, so that no order's rows appear on both sides, but ignores time. **Customer-grouped random** splits at the customer level, ignoring time, answering the question of how well a model predicts for customers it has never seen. **Chronological, orders intact, returning customers allowed** — the paper's primary estimand — sorts by decision time and holds out the most recent 20% of the data, answering the practically relevant question of how well the model predicts future orders in the firm's existing customer population, a population that legitimately includes returning customers whose own earlier orders are, correctly, present in training. **Chronological, new customers evaluated separately** is a secondary estimand restricted, within the same chronological test window, to customers absent from training, answering a harder, transfer-oriented question. Because DataCo's row is a line item and Olist's row is an order, row-level and order-grouped random splits coincide numerically for Olist, a structural fact the paper's design accounts for rather than treats as an oversight (Section 4.3).

Training size is matched, in unique orders, across the four non-secondary designs by subsampling each design's larger training pools down to the smallest common order count, so that observed contrasts are not confounded with training-set size; the chronological secondary estimand reuses the matched chronological-primary training set, varying only which rows of the held-out window are scored. All preprocessing (median/mode imputation, standardisation, one-hot encoding) is fit within each design's own training fold, never on the full dataset.

## 4.3 Models and tuning

Three model families are compared — L2-regularised logistic regression, a random forest, and a gradient-boosted tree ensemble (LightGBM) — alongside two domain-motivated single-feature baselines per dataset (shipping mode and scheduled days for DataCo; promised delivery window and customer region for Olist), which exist as a floor against which the full models are judged, not as competitors in their own right. Every model family receives the same tuning budget: a two-configuration grid per family, selected by a two-fold time-aware inner validation (`TimeSeriesSplit` on rows sorted by decision time within the training fold) rather than ordinary cross-validation, so that even a design whose outer split is random does not benefit from an inner validation procedure that itself leaks future information into hyperparameter selection.

## 4.4 Fixed-test-set mechanism experiments

To move from description to mechanism identification, the test set for a designated evaluation period is held completely fixed while only the training set is varied across four conditions, with training size held constant throughout: **A**, past data only, serving as the reference/primary-setting condition; **B**, past data plus other, non-test orders drawn from the same period as the test window, isolating the effect of time coverage without any group overlap; **C** (DataCo only, exploiting the order-line-item structure), condition B's training set plus a controlled, stepped share (0% to 100%) of the test orders' own sibling line items, isolating the effect of order-level group overlap by dose; and **D**, past data only, but with the test customers' own earlier orders guaranteed present in training, isolating the value of legitimate customer history as distinct from illegitimate group overlap. The design is repeated across four evaluation periods for DataCo and three for Olist (condition C omitted for Olist, since row and order coincide there, per Section 4.2). A single fixed model configuration (LightGBM) is used throughout each dataset's battery, so that the dose-response, where observed, cannot be attributed to tuning variation across conditions.

## 4.5 Simulation with known mechanisms

A complementary, fully synthetic design generates semi-realistic order-structured populations in which the data-generating process is set by the experimenter and therefore known exactly. Four binary factors are crossed in a complete 2⁴ factorial: **W**, a within-order random effect shared by an order's sibling rows, operationalised through an observable per-row "fingerprint" of that effect (reflecting the structural fact, confirmed directly in DataCo, that an order's covariates and label are shared across its line items, so a flexible model that has seen one sibling in training can look up, rather than generalise, the outcome for another); **S**, a covariate-distribution shift applied only to a later, "future" segment of the simulated population; **R**, a base-rate (intercept) shift applied to the same future segment; and **P**, a shift in the coefficient linking a "promise" covariate to the outcome, applied to the same segment. Three test-set constructions drawn from the same underlying population — row-random, order-grouped random, and chronological — allow two contrasts to be computed: random-vs-grouped, expected to track W; and grouped-vs-chronological, expected to track S, R and P jointly. Sixteen factorial cells (each combination of the four factors switched on or off) are each replicated eight times at a moderate sample size, and compared against a single very-large-sample reference run per cell, treated as the population-level truth; the gap between a replicate's estimated contrast and the reference run's contrast is the recovery error, the quantity that answers whether the standard descriptive contrasts, as typically computed in practice, actually recover the magnitude of the mechanism that produced them, or merely its sign.

## 4.6 Rolling-origin reliability analysis

Ten expanding-window forecast origins are constructed per dataset (minimum training fraction 40% of the chronologically sorted data, roughly 6–8% of the data scored as the test window at each origin), with a fixed model configuration (LightGBM) refit at each origin on all data available up to that point. At each origin we record two families of quantities: discrimination and calibration on that origin's test window, and a set of origin-level covariates describing how the population has moved relative to the training data underlying that origin — the population stability index and standardised mean shift for a small set of operationally meaningful numeric features, the change in lateness prevalence, the change in the mean promised delivery window, and the change in mean actual fulfilment time. The dependent variable in the paper's H5 regression is the *change* in AUC (and in the magnitude of calibration-in-the-large) from one origin's test window to the following origin's, regressed on the *current* origin's drift and prevalence-change covariates — testing whether today's measured drift is informative about tomorrow's performance change, which is the practically relevant question for a monitoring system, rather than whether drift and performance are merely contemporaneously correlated. For Olist specifically, an auxiliary random-forest regressor is trained at each origin to predict actual fulfilment time from operational covariates with the promised delivery window deliberately excluded; a stable or improving mean absolute error from this auxiliary model, even as late-delivery classification performance moves, is evidence that any classification deterioration is attributable to a shift in the promise itself rather than to a decline in the firm's underlying operational predictability — the distinction Hinder et al. (2024b) identify as the harder, more practically consequential half of drift analysis.

## 4.7 Uncertainty quantification and multiple-testing control

Every discrimination metric reported with an interval uses a paired cluster bootstrap (400–1,000 resamples depending on the analysis), resampling whole order clusters with replacement rather than individual rows, following Field and Welsh (2007); chronological comparisons additionally draw on weekly-block resampling in the spirit of Künsch (1989) where blocks, rather than individual observations or clusters, are the resampling unit. Repeated random splits are never treated as independent replications of a performance estimate, since they are not. Equivalence verdicts (superior / inferior / equivalent / inconclusive) follow Lakens (2017) directly: a contrast's interval entirely above the registered margin δ is superior, entirely below −δ is inferior, entirely within ±δ is equivalent, and a straddling interval is inconclusive rather than, as a p-value-only approach would report it, simply "significant" or "not significant." Across the multiple model families tested within each hypothesis, Holm's (1979) step-down procedure controls the family-wise error rate; Holm's method is valid under arbitrary dependence among the underlying test statistics, which matters here because the three model families' test statistics are computed on overlapping, not independent, test sets.

A reader familiar with the clinical and biostatistical literature on comparing correlated ROC curves may reasonably ask why this paper does not use DeLong, DeLong and Clarke-Pearson's (1988) nonparametric covariance-based test, the standard tool for comparing two AUCs estimated on the same subjects. The DeLong test answers a narrower question than the one this paper asks: it is designed for two AUCs computed on the *same* test set, differing only in which model produced the predictions, and its covariance estimator explicitly assumes the two sets of predictions share a common set of underlying subjects. The comparisons at the centre of this paper — row-random AUC versus order-grouped AUC, chronological AUC versus order-grouped AUC — are computed on *different* test sets, each produced by a different splitting procedure and each containing a different, dependently structured set of order clusters, so the DeLong covariance structure does not apply directly; the paired cluster bootstrap used throughout instead resamples each design's own cluster structure and computes the resulting difference distribution empirically, which generalises correctly to this paper's between-design comparisons in a way a same-subject test cannot.

## 4.9 Reproducibility

Every script, model configuration, random seed and intermediate result underlying this paper is released publicly, consistent with the proposal's commitment to open reporting. A fixed random seed (20261015) governs every stochastic element of the pipeline — train/test splitting, model initialisation, bootstrap resampling, and the simulation's data-generating draws — so that the exact numbers reported in Section 5 can be regenerated from the released code without re-estimation uncertainty confounding a replication attempt with genuine sampling variability. The registered analysis plan, including the hypotheses, equivalence margins and statistical tests stated in Section 3, was fixed before the final evaluation on the datasets' held-out evaluation periods; development and threshold-setting were confined to an early subset of the DataCo data and to the simulation study alone, so that the final confirmatory numbers reported for both datasets' primary evaluation windows were not used, directly or indirectly, to tune any analytic choice reported here.

## 4.8 Governance-gate integration

Every fitted (validation design, model family) combination from Section 4.2–4.3 is additionally passed through the BDP Model Gate (v0.6.0), an open-source automated pre-deployment governance tool that runs a battery of validation, performance, calibration, compliance and security checks and returns a PASS / NEEDS_REVIEW / BLOCKED verdict (Oden, 2026). The gate is run with its validation-category thresholds (leakage ratio, split overlap, feature drift) left at default, since these are exactly the checks this paper's own research questions concern, while its performance and security thresholds — calibrated by the tool's authors to a deployment-readiness standard rather than a research one — are relaxed from a minimum AUC of 0.8 and a maximum adversarial flip rate of 5% to 0.6 and 25% respectively, so that the gate's verdicts reflect validation-methodology findings rather than an unrelated judgement about whether a research-stage model with a narrow, shared tuning budget is fit for production. A structured model card declaring the validation strategy used (random, grouped, or out-of-time) is supplied with each call so that the gate's validation-strategy check can return a substantive verdict rather than reporting "not applicable" for lack of a declared strategy. Because several of the gate's checks (notably feature-level leakage and drift detection) scale poorly with full-dataset row counts, rows passed to the gate are capped at 4,000 per side; this does not affect the paper's own performance numbers, which are computed on the full evaluation set before the gate is ever called, and the gate's role throughout is descriptive corroboration of the paper's own findings, not a substitute source of evidence for them.

# 5. Results

## 5.1 Data audit confirms the registered population and outcome definitions

Applying the registered definitions to the raw data reproduced the proposal's preliminary signals exactly: 7,754 DataCo rows (4.30%) coded as cancelled; 100.0% of DataCo orders sharing a single label across every line item; a 100.0% late rate among non-cancelled First Class shipments; and an Olist monthly late-rate range of 1.4–21.4% in the primary window. The exact reproduction of these figures across an independent implementation of the population logic provides direct evidence that the population and outcome definitions were implemented as specified, rather than approximated.

## 5.2 Validation design materially changes the estimate, and which dimension it changes differs by dataset

On DataCo, row-random validation is the clear outlier: gradient boosting's AUC falls from 0.777 under row-random validation to 0.744 under order-grouped validation, 0.742 under customer-grouped validation, and 0.745 under chronological (primary) validation — the latter three agreeing with each other to within 0.003 AUC regardless of model family. DataCo's performance estimates are, in other words, overwhelmingly sensitive to whether orders are kept intact across the split, and essentially insensitive to whether the split additionally respects time, once group integrity is preserved. Olist shows close to the opposite pattern: the three random-design estimates (row, order-grouped, customer-grouped) agree closely with each other for every model family (gradient boosting: 0.776, 0.779, 0.782), but the chronological design is a materially different regime. Gradient boosting's chronological AUC (0.718) sits only modestly below its random-design estimates, but the random forest's chronological AUC is 0.487 — below the chance level of 0.5 — against 0.759–0.770 under the three random designs, and the single-feature promised-delivery-window baseline, which underperforms under every random design (AUC 0.54–0.56), jumps to 0.797 under chronological evaluation, accompanied by a calibration slope of 11.9, indicating severe systematic overconfidence rather than a genuinely improved model. Olist's temporal instability, localised in Section 5.7 to a 2018 contraction in the promised delivery window, is sufficient to invert a well-performing random forest into a worse-than-chance classifier purely as an artefact of which validation design is used to measure it.

## 5.4 Beyond AUC: average precision, Brier score, and the asymmetry between discrimination and calibration gaps

Average precision tracks AUC closely across every design and model family on both datasets — the ranking of designs by average precision never disagrees with the ranking by AUC for any model family — confirming that the paper's discrimination-based findings in Section 5.2 are not an artefact of AUC specifically. The Brier score (lower is better) tells a more separable story. For the real model families, Brier moves with AUC as expected: DataCo's Brier scores sit in a narrow 0.183–0.208 band that tracks the AUC pattern in Section 5.2 closely, and Olist's Brier scores are, if anything, *lowest* (best) under chronological evaluation for every model family (0.049–0.053) despite that design's catastrophic AUC for random forest — a reminder that Brier score conflates discrimination and calibration into a single number and can mask a severe discrimination failure when the outcome's base rate itself has shifted favourably (Olist's chronological test window has a lower late-delivery prevalence than its random-design counterparts, mechanically lowering Brier for any model that predicts close to the base rate, including a poorly discriminating one). The baselines make the discrimination/calibration asymmetry starkest: Olist's promised-delivery-window baseline achieves the dataset's *best* raw AUC of any model under chronological evaluation (0.797) while its calibration slope of 11.9 is the worst value recorded anywhere in this study, a combination that would mislead any practitioner who screens candidate models on discrimination alone, exactly the failure mode Van Calster et al. (2019) describe as calibration's comparative neglect in applied practice.

## 5.5 Registered hypothesis tests: confirmation is model-family-dependent, not universal

H1 (DataCo, row-random AUC exceeds order-grouped AUC by more than δ = 0.01) is confirmed decisively for gradient boosting (+0.032, 95% CI [0.020, 0.043], Holm-adjusted p = 0.006, verdict superior) but only directionally, not conclusively beyond the registered margin, for logistic regression (+0.021, [0.009, 0.032], inconclusive) and random forest (+0.015, [0.003, 0.027], inconclusive). H2 (Olist, chronological AUC is lower than order-grouped AUC by more than δ) is confirmed strongly for both tree ensembles — random forest, −0.277 [−0.300, −0.254], inferior; gradient boosting, −0.065 [−0.083, −0.045], inferior — but logistic regression moves in the *opposite* direction, +0.029 [0.010, 0.048], verdict superior to H2's registered expectation. The single model family robust enough to the 2018 promise-window shift to show a *higher*, not lower, chronological estimate is the one with the least capacity to overfit the shifting relationship in the first place. H6 (model rankings change between row-random and the primary design) is supported descriptively — two of three model families change rank position on both datasets — but Kendall's τ, computed on only three rankable items, carries a bootstrap interval spanning the entire [−1, 1] range on both datasets, and we report this honestly as an underpowered test rather than claim statistical confirmation the data cannot support.

## 5.6 The fixed-test-set dose-response experiment isolates group overlap as the mechanism, cleanly

Holding DataCo's test set and training-set size fixed across four evaluation periods, and varying only the share of test orders' sibling line items permitted into training, produces an almost perfectly linear dose-response: mean AUC across the four periods rises from 0.739 at 0% overlap (statistically indistinguishable from the 0.741 obtained under condition A, past-data-only, confirming that time coverage alone contributes essentially nothing) to 0.966 at 100% overlap. The pooled slope test gives slope = 0.229 AUC per unit overlap share, r = 0.994, p = 1.0 × 10⁻²² — the paper's single cleanest result, and one that could not have been obtained from a two-point descriptive comparison of "grouped" against "ungrouped": the monotonic, near-linear dose-response is itself the evidence that group overlap, specifically, rather than some unobserved confound correlated with grouping, drives the gap. Condition D — guaranteeing the test customers' own earlier orders are present in training, a form of group overlap that is legitimate rather than leaked, since it reflects information genuinely available before the decision — scores 0.729, statistically indistinguishable from condition A and far from condition C's elevated scores at any positive overlap share, demonstrating that the mechanism is specifically about information a model should not have, not about group overlap generically. On Olist, where condition C does not apply, giving the model contemporaneous non-test orders from the same period (condition B) raises mean AUC from 0.598 to 0.636, a pattern opposite to DataCo's, consistent with Olist's substantially greater temporal instability: on a dataset where the relationship between features and outcome moves over time, recent information is valuable regardless of whether it comes from the specific customers being evaluated, whereas on DataCo, where the relationship is stable but group-dependent, recency contributes almost nothing.

## 5.7 The factorial simulation confirms the mechanisms but reveals systematic recovery bias

The simulation's random-vs-grouped contrast, averaged across all cells with the within-order dependence factor W switched off, is −0.003 — statistically indistinguishable from zero — against +0.118 when W is switched on, a clean, large separation that required deliberately encoding W as a factor a sufficiently flexible model (here, a random forest) can exploit through a near-identifying covariate shared by sibling rows; a well-specified linear model, by contrast, generalises any smooth relationship between such a covariate and the outcome to unseen groups just as well as to seen ones, and so shows no contrast at all under the same design, a finding that is itself informative about which model families are structurally exposed to this mechanism (Section 6.2). The grouped-vs-chronological contrast rises monotonically with the number of temporal factors (S, R, P) switched on, from 0.034 with none active to 0.083 with all three active, with the single largest marginal jump attributable to P, the shift in the promise-outcome relationship — mirroring the paper's own empirical finding that a changing service promise, not a generic covariate shift, is the sharpest driver of chronological deterioration. Recovery error, however, is not centred on zero: the modest-sample estimate of the random-vs-grouped contrast systematically *overestimates* the large-sample truth by a mean of 0.079 when W is active, and the grouped-vs-chronological contrast is systematically *underestimated*, by roughly 0.02–0.03, once any temporal factor is active. Both biases are directionally consistent across all sixteen factorial cells, which argues against treating them as sampling noise and in favour of treating a single small mechanism experiment's point estimate as a biased, not merely noisy, guide to the population-level effect size.

## 5.8 Reliability over time: DataCo drifts without deteriorating; Olist's single largest drift coincides with a real episode

Across DataCo's ten rolling-origin windows, AUC is stable (0.731–0.758) and mean PSI stays low throughout (0.0002–0.009) with a mild rise at the final two origins; the regression of the *change* in next-period AUC on the current origin's PSI gives a positive but non-significant slope (1.94, r = 0.54, p = 0.135, n = 9) — and, concretely, the two origins with the highest measured drift are followed by *improving*, not worsening, performance, a direct, dataset-level instance of Eck et al.'s (2022) drift-without-deterioration finding. Olist's drift is an order of magnitude larger and far more volatile (PSI range 0.011–0.486 against DataCo's 0.0002–0.009), and the regression of next-period AUC change on PSI is directionally consistent with "more drift, worse next-period performance" (slope = −0.125, r = −0.25) but, again, not significant at n = 9 origins (p = 0.523); we report both regressions' non-significance plainly rather than lean on the point estimates' direction alone. The single largest-drift origin (PSI = 0.42, at the series' end) coincides with the same period implicated in Section 5.2's random-forest collapse. The auxiliary operational model, trained to predict actual fulfilment time from operational covariates with the promised window excluded, resolves the mechanism: its mean absolute error *improves* across the series, from 5.95 to 3.66 days, even as the mean promised window collapses from 27.3 to 14.6 days and mean actual fulfilment time falls from 14.5 to 7.2 days. Olist's operations got faster and more predictable over this period, not less; the classification collapse documented in Section 5.2 is attributable entirely to the promise shrinking faster than delivery times improved, so that orders cross from "on time" to "late" purely because the threshold moved, a distinction invisible to any analysis that reports "the model degraded" without separating the promise from the operation it is promising to meet.

## 5.8 Governance-gate verdicts independently corroborate the mechanism, and surface one driver the paper's own drift features missed

Run independently across every (design, model) combination, the gate's verdicts align closely with the paper's own findings without having been built to reproduce them: on Olist, every random design passes cleanly for every real model family, and every chronological design degrades to NEEDS_REVIEW or BLOCKED; on DataCo, random forest alone is BLOCKED under chronological evaluation, consistent with its markedly worse calibration slope (2.89–3.25) under that design relative to the other model families. Examining what specifically triggered the DataCo block is informative beyond corroboration: the gate's feature-drift check flags Order State, Order Country, Order Region and Market — geographic composition — as the features that moved most between training and the chronological test window (total variation distance 0.52–0.57 against a 0.15 threshold), a driver this paper's own rolling-origin drift features (Section 4.6, which tracked scheduled days, sales and discount rate) were not monitoring at all. On Olist, the gate's drift check independently flags `promised_days` and `purchase_month` as the two most-shifted features, directly corroborating Section 5.7's mechanism without having been told what that mechanism was. One flagged result is a methodological caution rather than a finding: the single-feature promised-delivery-window baseline triggers a 98.5% "duplicate rows" flag from the gate's generic split-overlap check, an artefact of comparing rows on one low-cardinality integer column — most orders share the same promised-day value — rather than evidence of genuine leakage, underscoring that a governance check's output is only interpretable in light of the feature set it was computed against (Section 6.5).

# 6. Discussion

## 6.1 The validation-design effect is not one effect

The paper's results refuse a single, portable "correction factor" for validation bias, and that refusal is itself the main methodological finding. DataCo's bias is almost entirely a group-dependence phenomenon, nearly orthogonal to time; Olist's is almost entirely a temporal phenomenon, essentially orthogonal to grouping (row and order coincide there by construction, so grouping cannot be separately implicated, but the random designs' close mutual agreement shows grouping is not where Olist's instability lives). A practitioner who has internalised "always use a grouped split" from one literature, or "always validate out-of-time" from another, will handle one of these two datasets correctly and the other one by accident. The paper's broader implication is that the correct validation design is an empirical question about the specific dependence and non-stationarity structure of the data at hand, not a single best practice importable wholesale from a different domain's methodological literature — precisely the conclusion Roberts et al. (2017) reach for ecological data, now extended with direct supply chain evidence.

## 6.2 Why model family mediates the mechanism, and what that implies for comparing models across studies

H1 and H2's model-family-dependent confirmation, and the simulation's demonstration that a well-specified linear model shows no random-vs-grouped contrast at all under a design where a flexible model shows a large one, together point to a mechanism that is not solely a property of the data but an interaction between the data's dependence structure and the model's capacity to exploit it. A model that can effectively memorise a near-duplicate row's label — which requires enough flexibility to carve out decision regions narrow enough to correspond to an individual group, something linear models with globally smooth decision boundaries generally cannot do but tree ensembles, especially with many trees and little regularisation, generally can — will show inflated performance under group overlap in a way a smoother model will not, even on the identical dataset and the identical overlap. Equally, H2's logistic-regression result — the one model family robust enough to a changing service promise to score *higher*, not lower, under chronological evaluation — suggests that a smoother model's relative disadvantage under random validation (it typically achieves lower AUC than the tree ensembles throughout this paper) can reverse into a relative *advantage* once temporal robustness, not peak in-sample discrimination, is the quantity that matters for a deployed system. A model comparison conducted only under random validation will not merely misstate each model's absolute performance; it can misstate their *relative order*, a possibility directly realised in this paper's own H6 results and one that should give pause to any published comparison of model families that reports only a single random-split benchmark.

## 6.3 Drift, deterioration, and the promise as a feature, not a label artefact

Section 5.7's auxiliary-model result reframes what "temporal drift" means for a service-promise-based outcome. A naive monitoring setup that tracks only the late/on-time label's prevalence, or only the model's AUC, would have reported Olist's 2018 episode as unexplained performance degradation. Decomposing the outcome into its two structural components — the promise and the actual fulfilment relative to it — shows that the firm's underlying operational capability was, if anything, improving over the same window; the model failed because the definition of success it had learned (what counts as "on time" given a roughly three-to-four-week promise) stopped matching the definition in force at evaluation time (a promise that had contracted to roughly two weeks). This is a retraining problem with a specific, actionable diagnosis — the promise changed, operations did not — rather than a generic "the model is stale" diagnosis that would suggest only "retrain more often" as a remedy, when the more precise remedy is to monitor the promise distribution itself as a leading indicator, independent of any drift statistic computed on the model's other features.

## 6.4 Underpowered tests are findings, not failures to report

Both H6's Kendall's τ and the O4 rolling-origin regressions are, by the paper's own account, statistically underpowered: three model families provide too few paired ranks for τ's bootstrap interval to exclude much of anything, and nine rolling-origin windows are too few for a regression's p-value to be trusted at face value. We resist the temptation, common in applied work under publication or completion pressure, either to omit these tests because they "didn't work" or to report only their point estimates without the accompanying interval that reveals how little those point estimates constrain. A test that is honestly reported as underpowered, alongside the raw descriptive pattern it was meant to formalise (two of three model families change rank; drift and next-period AUC move in the hypothesised direction more often than not), is more useful to a reader deciding how much weight to place on the finding than either suppressing the result or overstating its certainty would be. We flag this explicitly as a template for how this paper's own O5 reporting protocol (Section 6.6) should itself be read by a reader applying it: the protocol's ninth point, that a governance check's output is only interpretable against the feature set it was computed on, applies with equal force to the present paper's own hypothesis tests and their sample sizes.

## 6.5 What an automated gate adds, and what it still requires from a human reader

The gate's independent corroboration of this paper's central findings — the clean PASS/chronological-BLOCKED split on Olist, the geographic-drift flag on DataCo the paper's own analysis had not been tracking — is a genuine demonstration of the value automated governance tooling of the kind Mitchell et al. (2019) and Raji et al. (2020) advocate for can add to a validation-methodology study, since the gate was configured without knowledge of this paper's hypotheses and still surfaced consistent evidence. But the 98.5% "duplicate rows" false alarm on a single low-cardinality baseline feature is an equally genuine demonstration of the tool's limits: a generic statistical check, applied without domain judgement about what the feature set it is running against actually represents, can manufacture an apparent leakage signal from nothing more than coarse feature granularity. Governance tooling of this kind is best read as a second, independent source of evidence to be interpreted alongside a study's own domain knowledge, not as a replacement for it, and definitely not as a verdict a non-specialist reader should accept uncritically simply because it is automated and quantitative.

## 6.6 Situating the mechanism within the leakage taxonomy, and the cost of checking for it

Kapoor and Narayanan's (2023) taxonomy of eight leakage types gives a useful frame for stating precisely what this paper's central mechanism is, and is not. Their taxonomy distinguishes, among other categories, leakage from a non-independence between training and test samples (their category covering duplicated or near-duplicated observations and samples drawn from the same underlying unit) from leakage via features that encode the target directly. The mechanism this paper isolates — DataCo's sibling line items, Olist's repeat customers — belongs squarely to the first category: it is a non-independence leakage, not a feature-encodes-target leakage, and the distinction matters practically, because the remedies differ. Feature-encodes-target leakage is fixed by removing or recomputing a specific column; non-independence leakage cannot be fixed at the feature level at all; it requires changing the sampling unit of the split itself, from the row to the group the row belongs to, which is precisely what the paper's order-grouped and customer-grouped designs do and what a practitioner auditing a pipeline for leaked *features* alone would never catch, because no single feature is doing anything wrong. This is also why a feature-level leakage check, of the kind the governance gate in Section 4.8 runs by default (comparing each single feature's own predictive power against the whole model's), did not flag this paper's own DataCo dose-response mechanism directly in any of the gate reports (Section 5.8): the mechanism does not live in a feature, it lives in the sampling unit, and a check built to catch the first kind of leakage is structurally unable to catch the second.

This distinction also bears on cost. A feature-level leakage audit is computationally cheap — it requires fitting one single-feature model per candidate feature and comparing its performance to the full model, work the governance gate already performs in well under a second per run in this paper's pipeline. A non-independence audit of the kind this paper runs — the fixed-test-set dose-response experiment of Section 4.4 — is substantially more expensive: it requires refitting a model once per overlap-share step, per evaluation period, and the paper's own DataCo battery alone comprised 36 full model fits across four periods and nine conditions-and-overlap-share combinations. For a firm without a research team's time budget, the practical recommendation this cost asymmetry implies is not "always run a full dose-response experiment before trusting a performance number," which is unrealistic as a routine practice, but a cheaper proxy that is realistic: report the number of unique groups (orders, customers) in the training and test sets alongside the row counts, since a test set whose distinct-group count is far smaller than its row count is the single cheapest observable signal that a non-independence leakage mechanism of the kind documented here could be present, well before running the full experiment that would prove it.

## 6.7 Toward a reporting protocol

The nine-point minimum-evidence protocol in Section 5's companion results report is not a generic checklist asserted from first principles; each point traces to a specific result in this paper. State the estimand before the number, because Section 5.2 shows the same model's AUC moving by as much as 0.29 depending on which of five designs produced it. Report the group-overlap share continuously, not as a binary "grouped: yes/no," because Section 5.5's dose-response is quasi-linear across the entire 0–100% range, not a step function. Report the model family alongside any validation-design contrast, because Section 5.4 shows the same design swap reversing sign across model families. Report the promised-window distribution at train and test time specifically, not merely a date range, because Section 5.7 shows that a generic date-range disclosure would have missed the actual mechanism entirely. Run a dose-response check, not a two-point comparison, before attributing a gap to leakage, because a two-point comparison cannot distinguish a true leakage mechanism from an unlucky test-set draw, while a monotonic dose-response across controlled steps can. Use dependence-respecting resampling and pre-registered equivalence margins, because Section 5.4's H1 result shows two model families with the same-signed, similarly-sized point estimate receiving opposite verdicts once the margin and the interval are both taken seriously. Run a governance gate on every design used, not only the one behind a headline figure, because Section 5.8's cleanest corroboration came precisely from comparing the gate's verdicts across designs. Do not treat drift as inherently a deterioration signal, because Section 5.7's DataCo result shows the opposite pattern directly. And interpret a generic automated check against the feature set it ran on, because Section 5.8's false alarm would otherwise be read as a genuine leakage finding.

# 7. Limitations

Several limitations qualify these results and should condition how far they generalise. First, DataCo's outcome is, as this paper's own audit confirms, close to deterministic given order identity, a structural property that makes the dataset an excellent instrument for isolating group-dependence leakage but means DataCo-specific magnitudes (the 0.229 dose-response slope in particular) should not be read as a general estimate of how large group-overlap leakage is "in supply chain data" generally; a dataset with a noisier, order-level-but-not-perfectly-determined outcome would likely show a smaller, though almost certainly still present, effect. Second, the rolling-origin regressions (Section 5.7) and the model-ranking test (Section 5.4, H6) are both explicitly underpowered at the sample sizes available (nine origins; three model families) and are reported, deliberately, as directionally informative rather than statistically conclusive; a study with a longer available history, more forecast origins, or more model families compared would strengthen both. Third, the factorial simulation's recovery-error estimates rest on eight replicates per cell at a single fixed non-reference sample size; a denser replicate count, and a wider range of reference sample sizes, would better characterise how the bias documented in Section 5.6 scales with sample size, something the present design can show exists but cannot fully characterise. Fourth, the mechanism experiments and the rolling-origin analysis both rely on a single model family (gradient boosting) for tractability; while Section 5.4's model-family-dependent hypothesis results argue strongly that the mechanism identified for one model family need not generalise to another, the paper has not re-run the full mechanism and rolling-origin batteries across all three model families, and a reader should not assume the specific numeric slopes reported in Sections 5.5 and 5.7 would be identical for logistic regression or random forest. Fifth, the governance-gate integration (Section 4.8) relaxes the tool's deployment-grade performance and security thresholds to suit a research setting and caps the rows passed to the gate for tractability; both choices were necessary to obtain results in reasonable time but mean the gate's verdicts reported here should not be read as a claim about whether these specific models would pass the tool's thresholds in an unmodified, production configuration. Sixth, both datasets concern late-delivery prediction specifically; whether the same mechanisms, at similar magnitudes, hold for other common supply chain prediction tasks — demand forecasting, inventory risk, supplier default — is a question this paper's design cannot answer and leaves to future work. Finally, this paper evaluates automated governance checks only as a source of corroborating evidence; a systematic assessment of whether, and under what configuration, such a gate improves actual release decisions relative to a human reviewer working from the paper's own evidence alone is deliberately out of scope and reserved for a companion study.

# 8. Conclusion

Validation design is not a procedural footnote to be disposed of in one sentence of a methods section; across two supply chain datasets and three model families, it was capable of inflating a model's apparent performance by over three points of AUC through group-dependence leakage alone, of inverting a well-performing classifier into one that performs below chance through an unacknowledged temporal shift, and of reversing which of two models a comparison conducted under one validation design would recommend relative to another. Crucially, the paper's controlled dose-response and simulation experiments show that these are not merely two different numbers that happen to differ — a monotonic, near-linear response to a continuously controlled overlap share, and a simulation in which the true mechanism was set by the experimenter and recovered, with a documented and directional bias, by the standard descriptive contrast, both demonstrate that the usual practice of comparing one validation design's estimate against another is evidence of a real, specific, manipulable mechanism, not an artefact of two unlucky test-set draws. The paper's reporting protocol translates these findings into nine concrete requirements a performance claim for a supply chain model should meet before a practitioner relies on it. None of the nine requirements is exotic or expensive relative to standard practice; all nine are things this paper's own results show to matter, directly and often by a wide margin, for whether a reported number describes the model a firm is about to deploy or a different, better-behaved model that happens to share its name.
