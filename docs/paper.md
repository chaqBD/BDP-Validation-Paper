# Abstract

Automated pre-deployment governance tools promise to catch the validation defects — leakage, group-dependence overlap, schema mismatches — that a metrics-only review misses, but published evidence for this promise rarely separates three different things: what the tool's own named checks actually detect, how the way a team integrates the tool (row caps, thresholds, which inputs it is given) changes what those checks can see, and what measurable improvement, if any, an added check contributes over cheap alternatives. This paper separates all three directly, for the BDP Model Gate (v0.6.0) reviewing a late-delivery prediction model on two supply chain datasets (DataCo, order-line-item granularity; Olist, order granularity). We construct independently labelled review cases with known ground truth — a clean reference pipeline (training rows whose own outcome was not yet knowable by the release cutoff removed, using each dataset's own best-available timestamp precision), four injected defects, two legitimate distribution-change scenarios, plus a severity/seed sweep and eight supplementary rolling-origin periods per dataset not used to build either scenario case — and score every case with three model families under six comparator approaches: AUROC-only, metrics-only, an automated checklist, the stock gate, the stock gate extended with a group-identifier check, and a trivial check of whether any training row's own decision time falls on or after the release cutoff. Every result below is traceable to a uniquely tagged gate report; capped and uncapped checks are always run against the identical fitted model and prediction vector, never compared across separately generated runs.

**Stock-library capability.** The gate's own named checks catch a schema mismatch reliably (6/6 model-dataset combinations) and never fire on an injected combination leak (0/12) or on a group-overlap defect defined by non-identical records (0/8, the one defect type with no corresponding stock check at all). **Integration effects.** A 4,000-row sampling cap used for tractability understates the stock duplication check's own measured overlap by 42.6-fold on DataCo and 17.4-fold on Olist, measured against the identical fitted model and predictions at full scale; whether the capped check actually crosses its own blocking threshold for this battery's main instance differs by dataset (it does for Olist, not for DataCo), and a five-severity, three-seed sweep shows the check itself does not fire reliably below roughly 20% injected duplication on either dataset, even though the aggregate gate verdict is flagged at every severity tested from 2% upward — carried by unrelated drift and calibration checks, not by duplication detection. **Extension capability.** A group-identifier extension and an independent, identifier-free temporal-ordering check both flag every instance of the group-overlap and exact-duplicate defects in this battery; because every defect instance here moves a test-period row into training, no case in this design separates a group-identity violation from a temporal-ordering violation, so this battery cannot establish that the identifier-based extension adds detection the temporal check does not already provide. A direct timestamp-corruption test shows this gap in coverage matters differently by dataset: DataCo's non-time-aware extension is unaffected by a corrupted training-timestamp column, while Olist's time-aware extension is fooled by the identical corruption, exactly as the temporal check is. **Decision usefulness.** Against a genuinely subsequent, independently fitted rolling-origin period's own acceptance-threshold outcome (not a same-period metrics rule), the stock and extended gate flag 17 of 18 testable origin-pairs — achieving the same recall (0.8) as AUROC-only, metrics-only and the checklist, but at roughly a third the precision (0.24 vs. 0.67-0.80), because they flag almost everything regardless of whether the following period's model actually fails. We conclude that the gate's demonstrated value on this battery is confined to schema and duplication checks already matched or exceeded by a checklist costing two to four orders of magnitude less, that its group-overlap coverage is not shown to exceed a near-free temporal check, and that its aggregate verdict is a markedly worse predictor of a genuinely subsequent period's outcome than several cheaper alternatives tested against the same evidence.

**Keywords:** model governance; pre-deployment review; data leakage; fault injection; validation methodology; supply chain prediction

# 1. Introduction

A supply chain firm about to deploy a model that scores incoming orders for late-delivery risk faces a narrower, more concrete decision than "is this a good model": someone has to decide whether *this specific* trained model, evaluated in *this specific* way, is safe to put in front of operational decisions next week. The dominant way that decision gets made in practice is by reading a performance report — an AUROC number, maybe a calibration plot. This is adequate for catching a model that is simply not accurate enough. It is not adequate for catching the failure modes the methodological literature has spent over a decade documenting: a test set that leaks into training through a shared group identifier, a feature that encodes information from after the decision point, a schema mismatch between what a model was fitted on and what it is being scored against (Kaufman, Rosset, Perlich, & Stitelman, 2012; Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023). None of these failure modes are visible in an AUROC number on their own — several of them, leakage chief among them, make AUROC look *better*, not worse, which is precisely why a performance-only review cannot catch them.

Automated pre-deployment governance tools exist to close this gap. Model cards (Mitchell et al., 2019) standardise what a release report should contain; internal algorithmic auditing frameworks (Raji et al., 2020) formalise the process that should produce it; a newer generation of software packages — of which the BDP Model Gate used throughout this paper is one example — attempt to compute the relevant checks automatically and return a single PASS / NEEDS_REVIEW / BLOCKED verdict a release pipeline can act on. The promise is substantial: a firm gets the benefit of a specialist methodological review without needing a specialist methodologist on every release. Whether it delivers on that promise is rarely tested directly, and a typical demonstration runs a tool once on a real dataset and treats whatever verdict it returns as self-evidently meaningful. That is not evidence the tool catches what it is meant to catch, misses what a careful human would also miss, or avoids flagging things that were never actually wrong.

This paper makes a further methodological point that a single-run demonstration also cannot make: an aggregate PASS/NEEDS_REVIEW/BLOCKED verdict conflates at least three different questions that a prospective adopter needs answered separately. First, does the tool's own **named check** for a given failure mode actually fire when that failure mode is present — the stock library's intrinsic detection *capability*? Second, how does the way a team *integrates* the tool — what row cap it applies for tractability, what thresholds it configures, what optional inputs it supplies — change what that same check can see, independent of the library's own logic? Third, does a cheap, targeted *extension* add anything beyond what the stock checks and a lightweight non-specialist alternative already provide? And fourth, does any of this translate into better *decisions* — does a flag predict something a genuinely subsequent, previously unseen evaluation would also find wrong, or does it only predict itself? A study that reports a single BLOCKED-or-not number collapses all four into one figure and cannot separate a defect from a configuration choice, or a check's real detection from a reviewer's aggregate impression of "something got flagged." This paper keeps the four apart throughout, with traceable, uniquely tagged evidence behind each one.

This is a methodological move borrowed from software testing, where mutation and fault-injection studies measure whether a test suite actually detects the faults it is meant to detect (Andrews, Briand, & Labiche, 2005); Breck, Polyzotis, Roy, Whang, and Zinkevich (2019) apply a closely related idea to machine learning data-validation pipelines, building checks whose value is demonstrated by the specific anomalies they catch in production data. We adopt the same standard: construct cases with a known defect or an explicitly known absence of one, and measure detection, integration sensitivity, incremental extension value and predictive validity against a later, genuinely untouched period, not only same-period agreement between two rules built by the same team.

The paper's operational use case is stated once, up front: reviewing a late-delivery prediction model before it is released to score future orders, on two datasets (DataCo, Olist) whose reference pipelines — order-safe, chronologically ordered, with leakage-free predictors and a held-out final evaluation period — were established in a companion methodological study of validation design and are restated self-contained here (Section 4.2).

The paper's contributions are five, matched to the four-way distinction above plus the traceability discipline that makes the others trustworthy. **Stock capability**: the gate's named checks reliably catch a schema mismatch and never fire on an injected combination leak or a distinct-record group-overlap defect, across every model family tested including one with a pre-existing, defect-independent baseline problem. **Integration effects**: a row-sampling cap degrades the duplication check's sensitivity by a measured, dataset-specific factor, with the capped check's own threshold-crossing behaviour differing by dataset at this battery's actual severity and never reliably firing below roughly 20% duplication in a dedicated severity sweep — evidence a same-run, same-fit comparison makes directly comparable for the first time in this study's history. **Extension capability**: a group-identifier extension and a near-free temporal-ordering check both close the stock gate's group-overlap miss in this battery, and a direct timestamp-corruption test shows the two checks' robustness differs by dataset in a way this paper's own earlier draft had claimed without testing. **Decision usefulness**: measured against a genuinely subsequent rolling-origin period's own acceptance outcome, the stock and extended gate are the least precise of six approaches tested, despite matching the best recall. **Traceability**: every number in this paper is generated by a script that writes its result under a unique tag, with a manifest linking each row to the exact report it came from, specifically to prevent the kind of silent cross-version contamination an earlier draft of this battery was found to contain.

# 2. Literature review

## 2.1 Leakage as the central failure mode a release review must catch

Kaufman, Rosset, Perlich and Stitelman (2012) formalised data leakage as the introduction, deliberate or accidental, of information about the target that would not legitimately be available at decision time, and catalogued its common vectors: features computed over a window that includes the future, and test instances that are not truly independent of training instances because they share an unobserved common cause or a direct structural link such as group membership. Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) surveyed 17 research fields and found leakage, in one of eight taxonomised forms, implicated in 294 published papers; in their own re-analysis of a civil-war prediction application, correcting the leakage eliminated the claimed superiority of machine learning models over decades-old statistical baselines entirely. Both papers motivate this study's central design choice: if leakage is common, underdetected, and frequently invisible in the metric it is meant to corrupt, then a governance tool's value rests specifically on whether it can detect leakage a performance-only review cannot — a testable, not merely plausible, claim. Their taxonomy separates leakage from features that directly encode the target, from non-independence between training and test samples (duplicated or near-duplicated observations, or samples sharing an unobserved common unit), and from a *combination* of otherwise individually unremarkable features. This paper's four defects were chosen to span this taxonomy: the leaked-feature case instantiates the first category; exact-duplicate and group-overlap instantiate the second; and, as Section 6.5 argues, the leaked-feature case's mechanism of action sits closer to the taxonomy's third, least-discussed category than to its first.

## 2.2 Validation design as the context, not the subject, of this study

A companion methodological literature — Roberts et al. (2017) on cross-validation for dependent data, Bergmeir and Benítez (2012) and Tashman (2000) on temporal validation, Lakens (2017) on equivalence testing, Künsch (1989) and Field and Welsh (2007) on dependence-respecting resampling — establishes how severely an inappropriate validation design can bias a performance estimate. This paper's companion study applied that literature to the same two datasets used here and found that a naive row-level random split inflates a gradient-boosted model's AUROC by several points relative to an order-safe split, and that a changing service promise can collapse a tree ensemble's chronological AUROC below chance. Those findings motivate the present paper but are not re-derived here; Section 5.1 reports them only as context.

## 2.3 Model families, flexibility, and leakage's model-dependence

Random forests (Breiman, 2001) and gradient-boosted decision trees (here, LightGBM; Ke et al., 2017) can carve out a partition narrow enough to correspond to a single group, in effect memorising that group's label. A linearly parameterised model, such as the regularised logistic regression used alongside them, cannot do this in the same way. A defect that inflates a tree ensemble's apparent performance dramatically may leave a linear model's performance almost unchanged on identical data, so a governance check's apparent "miss" on one model family in isolation can be a property of that family's exposure to the defect, not the check itself — a distinction this paper's per-model-family, defect-specific reporting (Section 5.2) is built to preserve.

## 2.4 Calibration as a distinct, separately checkable failure mode

A model can rank cases correctly while every probability it outputs is systematically wrong, because discrimination and calibration are mathematically independent properties of a classifier (Van Calster, McLernon, van Smeden, Wynants, & Steyerberg, 2019). Platt (1999) and Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005) show different model families are natively miscalibrated in systematically different, recalibratable directions. A review tool that checks calibration alongside discrimination checks something a bare AUROC-only rule structurally cannot see.

## 2.5 Drift, deterioration, and what a review at a single point in time cannot see

Eck, Kabakci-Zorlu, Chen, Savard and Bao (2022) documented, with supply chain examples, that feature or prediction drift does not always precede or accompany a decline in realised performance; Hinder, Vaquet and Hammer (2024a, 2024b) distinguish detecting a distributional shift from explaining where and why it occurred. This paper's scenario cases and supplementary rolling-origin periods (Section 4.7) are built on this distinction: a legitimate distribution change is not, by itself, evidence a release should be blocked, and a tool that treats every detected shift as a defect is making a different, separately testable kind of error from one that misses a genuine defect — a pattern this paper's own evidence (Section 5.6) shows the stock gate falling into on the large majority of legitimate periods tested.

## 2.6 Machine learning in supply chain management: adoption without a commensurate review literature

Douaioui, Oucheikh, Benmoussa and Mabrouki (2024) reviewed 119 papers on machine learning for demand forecasting in supply chain management, documenting a steep rise in publication volume; Vlachos and Pulagam (2025) conducted a broader systematic review and catalogued the barriers — data quality, interpretability, organisational trust — that have slowed adoption. Neither treats pre-deployment review as a first-class methodological concern, and automated governance tooling is essentially absent from the supply-chain-specific literature. This paper addresses that gap by testing one representative tool against a stated, falsifiable standard.

## 2.7 Model governance, internal auditing, and the standard this paper holds the gate to

Mitchell et al. (2019) proposed model cards to prevent models from being deployed into contexts their evaluation did not cover; Raji et al. (2020) proposed an end-to-end internal algorithmic auditing framework. Both frame governance as a *process* that produces structured evidence, not merely a score. This paper holds the BDP Model Gate (Oden, 2026) to the same standard, treating its verdict as one output among several that must be jointly evaluated, since the headline verdict alone can conceal a genuine, relevant detection behind the same label as an irrelevant check firing (Section 5).

## 2.8 Fault injection as a test of detection capability, not merely existence

Andrews, Briand and Labiche (2005) found that carefully generated mutants track real-fault detection reasonably well, while hand-seeded faults behave differently and are often harder to detect than the real faults they approximate — a caution Section 7 returns to when discussing how far this paper's injected defects, severities and supplementary periods generalise. Breck, Polyzotis, Roy, Whang and Zinkevich (2019) built an analogous case for machine learning specifically. Both establish the principle this study applies to a governance gate: a checking tool's value is demonstrated by what it is shown to detect under controlled, independently labelled conditions, not by the plausibility of a single uncontrolled run.

# 3. Research questions and hypotheses

**RQ1 (stock capability) — Does the gate's own named check for a given defect type fire when that defect is present, independent of the aggregate verdict?** H1: the stock gate's named check for the group-overlap and exact-duplicate defects fires at a materially lower rate than for the schema-mismatch defect.

**RQ2 (integration effects) — Does the row-sampling cap used for tractability change what the stock gate's own check detects, holding the fitted model and predictions fixed?** H2: capped and uncapped checks, run against the identical fit, disagree on whether the exact-duplicate defect's relevant check fires, and the disagreement's magnitude is dataset-specific.

**RQ3 (extension capability) — Does a group-identifier extension add detection a near-free, identifier-free temporal-ordering check does not already provide, and is either check's value sensitive to whether the training-timestamp column can be trusted?** H3a: the extension detects the group-overlap and exact-duplicate defects at least as reliably as the stock gate. H3b: a temporal-integrity check detects the same two defects at least as reliably as the extension in this battery. H3c: the two checks' robustness to a corrupted training-timestamp column differs between DataCo's (non-time-aware) and Olist's (time-aware) extension policy.

**RQ4 (decision usefulness) — Does a flag on one rolling-origin period predict that a genuinely subsequent, independently fitted period will itself fail a common acceptance criterion?** H4: the stock and extended gate's flags are a worse predictor (lower precision at matched recall) of next-period acceptance failure than the cheaper comparators.

All hypotheses were stated, and all thresholds in Section 4.5 were frozen, before the battery in Section 4.3 was run. H3b, H3c and RQ4 were added in this round, directly motivated by inspecting *why* the extension and the aggregate verdict behaved as they did in an earlier draft; this is disclosed rather than presented as though hypothesised from the start. Section 5 reports each as a qualified finding: H1 is confirmed; H2 is confirmed with the sampling effect quantified precisely under a same-fit comparison; H3a and H3b are both confirmed, which narrows rather than strengthens the case for the extension specifically; H3c is confirmed, and asymmetrically, which narrows it further; H4 is confirmed.

# 4. Methods

## 4.1 Reference pipeline

For each dataset, the reference ("clean") pipeline is the chronological, order/customer-safe pipeline established in the companion validation-design study: decision time fixed at order placement (DataCo) or purchase (Olist), predictors restricted to those legitimately available at that time, a held-out final chronological test window, and model hyperparameters frozen from that study's own time-aware tuning (logistic regression `C=0.1`; random forest 150 trees, max depth 8, minimum leaf 20; LightGBM 150 trees, 15 leaves, learning rate 0.1), never re-tuned against this paper's own cases.

## 4.2 Datasets, populations, and feature availability (self-contained)

**DataCo Smart Supply Chain** (Constante, Silva, & Pereira, 2019) records order line items for a simulated/semi-synthetic supply chain retailer. The primary population — non-cancelled orders, 2015-01-01 to 2017-09-30 — is 164,401 line-item rows across 54,796 distinct orders, 57.3% late-delivery rate. Twenty-three predictors legitimately available at order-placement time are retained (twelve numeric: scheduled shipping days, order item discount and discount rate, item price, item quantity, sales, item total, product price, customer latitude/longitude, order month and day-of-week; eleven categorical: shipment type, product category, customer segment, customer state/country, market, order region/state/country, shipping mode, department). Columns recording information only knowable after the outcome are excluded by construction, as are direct identifiers, free text, and constant columns. DataCo's row is a line item, so a single order's line items share one decision time and one label by construction, which is what makes an order id the natural non-independence boundary for this dataset's group-overlap defect (Section 4.3).

**Olist** (Olist, 2018) records individual marketplace orders. The primary population — delivered orders with a promise date at least 14 days before the data cut-off, purchases 2017-01-01 to 2018-08-31, eight orders marked delivered but missing a recorded delivery date excluded as a data-quality artefact — is 96,180 orders across 93,078 distinct customers, 8.1% late-delivery rate. This is a retrospective, delivered-only construction: it conditions on an order having actually completed, which is standard for a post-hoc late-delivery audit but means the population itself already reflects which orders were resolved by the data's collection date, a limitation of the data source this paper does not attempt to correct. Seventeen predictors are retained, including the number of a customer's own **prior** orders — computed by counting only that same customer's orders with a strictly earlier decision time, so a repeat customer's feature value never reflects a future order — and payment aggregates (total payment value, instalment count) computed from the payment records attached to that same order at the time it was placed, not from any later-arriving payment event; Olist's payment data is recorded at checkout, so this is available at decision time by construction, a property this paper verified against the schema rather than assumed.

Both datasets' reference pipelines enforce that a training row's own outcome must have been genuinely resolvable by the release cutoff used to construct each case: a row whose shipment was still in transit at that cutoff has a label that was not actually knowable at the point the model would have been trained. For Olist, this uses the exact `order_delivered_customer_date` timestamp recorded in the source data. For DataCo, no such timestamp exists — the source data records fulfilment only as a whole-day count ("Days for shipping (real)"), so this paper reconstructs an approximate resolution time as decision time plus that day count, which is the coarsest precision the source data itself supports, not a simplification this paper's own code introduces. This removes 602 of 131,518 DataCo training rows (0.46%) and 2,080 of 76,904 Olist training rows (2.70%) from the reference pipeline before any defect is constructed on top of it.

## 4.3 The case battery

Seven cases per dataset, each independently labelled with ground truth, scored under three frozen model configurations (forty-two case-model combinations in total). Every case's held-out, scored test rows and its training rows are index-disjoint by construction and asserted to be so at run time, with one deliberate exception (exact duplication is, definitionally, not disjoint).

- **Clean reference** (no defect): the reference pipeline as defined above.
- **Defect: leaked feature.** A post-outcome predictor — actual fulfilment time, already excluded from the reference feature set — is added back and the model refit. Ground truth: target leakage present.
- **Defect: exact test-row duplication.** A fixed 20% of the held-out test rows are also inserted, unchanged, into training; these rows are, by definition, both trained on and scored. Ground truth: split overlap present.
- **Defect: related-order/customer overlap.** DataCo: for every test order with more than one line item, all but one (kept, scored) row are moved into training; the scored test set is reduced to one representative row per order. Olist: for every customer with more than one order inside the held-out test window, all but the earliest such order are moved into training. In both cases the moved rows are *different, distinct* records from the ones still being scored, not copies — the mechanism isolated is non-independence between distinct records sharing a group, not literal duplication. Ground truth: non-independence (group) overlap present. By construction, every row this defect moves into training carries a decision time at or after the held-out window's start — central to Section 5.4's finding.
- **Defect: invalid feature schema.** One feature column is dropped from the data handed to the review process only; model predictions are computed correctly beforehand from the true schema. Ground truth: schema mismatch present.
- **Scenario: drift window** (not a defect). The rolling-origin window each dataset's own companion analysis identified as highest-drift at that series' final origin (PSI 0.0090 DataCo; 0.42 Olist).
- **Scenario: prevalence-shift window** (not a defect). The rolling-origin window with the largest measured change in lateness prevalence, per dataset.

Both the primary chronological split and every rolling-origin window are constructed with a composite sort key — decision time first, group id second — so a cut never falls inside a single order's or customer's rows, including when two distinct groups are tied on decision time; zero group-id overlap between training and scored rows is asserted at run time for every split used here.

## 4.4 Six comparator approaches, with thresholds frozen in advance

**AUROC-only**: BLOCKED if AUROC falls below 0.65. **Metrics-only**: BLOCKED if AUROC falls below 0.65 or Brier exceeds 0.22; NEEDS_REVIEW if calibration slope falls outside [0.7, 1.4] — genuinely metrics-only, no schema, overlap or group information. **Automated checklist**: the metrics-only rule plus a schema check and an exact-row-overlap check via row hash on the full data (NEEDS_REVIEW at ≥1%, BLOCKED at ≥5%). **Stock gate**: BDP Model Gate v0.6.0, validation-category thresholds at default, performance/security thresholds relaxed to suit a research-stage model (minimum AUROC 0.6, maximum adversarial flip rate 25%), rows capped at 4,000 per side. **Extended gate**: the stock gate combined with one additional group-identifier check (Section 4.5). **Temporal integrity**: BLOCKED if any training row's own decision time falls on or after the held-out window's start, otherwise PASS — no declared identifier, no graduated threshold.

## 4.5 The extended check

The group-identifier overlap check is run with a policy declared once per dataset, before any case is built, and applied identically to every case. DataCo: the order id, the fraction of test order ids also in training, no time-awareness needed (an order has one decision time, so any split of its rows is illegitimate regardless of when it occurs). Olist: the customer id, checked *time-aware* — a training-side order only counts as overlap if its own decision time falls on or after the held-out window's start, so a customer's genuinely past orders do not count. Both are computed on the full index sets, flagged NEEDS_REVIEW at ≥1%, BLOCKED at ≥5%.

## 4.6 Why NEEDS_REVIEW is a routine outcome, not a diagnosis

Since version 0.6.0, twelve of the gate's checks bootstrap their statistic and route an interval straddling its threshold to NEEDS_REVIEW rather than a forced OK or failure (`uncertainty.on_uncertain = "review"`, the library's default). A research-stage model evaluated against a frozen 0.6 AUROC threshold will often produce an interval straddling that threshold by construction, so a substantial share of this battery's NEEDS_REVIEW verdicts reflect the gate correctly executing its own stated policy, not a defect being detected. Neither the aggregate BLOCKED verdict nor the aggregate NEEDS_REVIEW verdict is this paper's primary evidence of detection, however; the specific named check is (Section 4.8).

## 4.7 Supplementary evaluation: severities, periods, and a genuinely subsequent check

Three extensions beyond the single constructed instance per defect, none tuned against, nor used to adjust, any threshold in Section 4.4, each saved under a unique tag with a manifest linking every row to the exact gate report it came from (an earlier draft of this script instead reused the literal case name `defect_exact_duplicate` for every severity/seed instance, so all fifteen per-dataset runs, and potentially the main battery's own report for that case, shared one file on disk; this version cannot repeat that error, since every tag includes its own severity and seed):

1. **Severity/seed sweep**: five duplication levels (2%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 30% of the test set) crossed with three independently drawn seeds each, exact-duplicate only (the one defect with a free severity parameter), scored with LightGBM, extracting the stock gate's own `split_overlap` sub-check flag and fraction for every instance, not only the aggregate verdict.
2. **Supplementary rolling-origin periods**: eight origins per dataset not used by either hand-picked scenario case, scored the same way. These are **supplementary**, not genuinely unseen: their drift and prevalence characteristics were already computed in the companion study's O4 rolling-origin analysis before this battery existed. "Supplementary" here means "not used to build this battery's two scenario cases," and is reported as such throughout, not as "held-out" in the sense of previously unexamined data.
3. **Subsequent-period check**: for every rolling-origin period with a usable next period (9 of 10 origins per dataset, 18 origin-pairs pooled), whether each approach's flag on origin *i* predicts that origin *i+1*'s own, independently fitted model fails a common 0.65 AUROC acceptance criterion — the genuinely external check Section 4.8's same-period metrics corroboration cannot provide, since that corroboration uses the same period's own data.

A fourth check tests one specific claim directly rather than asserting it: whether the extension's demonstrated robustness to a corrupted or missing training-timestamp column (an advantage an earlier draft of this paper claimed in general) actually holds for both datasets' declared policies. Every training row's decision time is set to an arbitrarily early, safely-in-the-past value, and the extension and the temporal check are re-run on the group-overlap defect under each dataset's own policy (Section 5.5).

## 4.8 Three outcomes kept distinct, and the primary detection endpoint

Every case carries three separate pieces of information, reported separately and never collapsed: (a) **ground truth** — whether the case has an injected defect; (b) **check-specific detection** — whether the one named check relevant to that defect type fired (`target_leakage` for the leaked feature; the stock gate's `split_overlap` for exact duplication; `feature_contract` for the schema mismatch; the extension and, separately, the temporal check for group overlap — reported as separate columns, never OR'd together into one boolean), computed identically for every model family, including Olist's random forest; and (c) **same-period metrics corroboration** — whether the metrics-only rule, using no schema, overlap or group information, also flags the same case. (c) is corroboration by a prespecified rule evaluated on the same period, not independent confirmation that a release recommendation was correct — both numbers come from the same data and the same thresholds this paper itself froze. Section 4.7's subsequent-period check provides the genuinely external validation (c) cannot.

One (dataset, model) pair — Olist's random forest — has a pre-existing, defect-independent AUROC problem documented in the companion study (a 2018 service-promise contraction its frozen configuration does not capture) that depresses its AUROC on every case, including the clean reference. Because the primary detection endpoint (b) asks whether a *named check* fired, not whether the *aggregate verdict* was BLOCKED, this pairing's results are reported alongside every other model's throughout Section 5 rather than excluded.

# 5. Results

## 5.1 Context from the companion study: why a review process is needed at all

The companion validation-design study found that a naive row-random split inflates a gradient-boosted model's AUROC on DataCo by 0.032 (95% CI [0.020, 0.043]) relative to an order-safe split, with the inflation tracking the share of leaked sibling rows almost perfectly (slope 0.229, r = 0.994); and that a changing service promise collapses a random forest's chronological AUROC on Olist from 0.78 to 0.49 — below chance. Reported here only as context for why a pre-deployment review process is needed at all, before asking whether this specific tool provides it.

## 5.2 Stock capability: what the named checks actually detect

Figure 1 reports the complete approach × defect × dataset detection table, with the stock gate's own named check, the extension and the temporal-integrity check always kept as separate columns — never combined into a single "detected" boolean, the way an earlier draft of this analysis did for the group-overlap defect specifically.

Two results are unambiguous and model-independent. `feature_contract` fires on every single invalid-schema case, in every model family, in both datasets (6/6) — matched exactly by the automated checklist's own schema check, and the one check in this battery whose reliability does not depend on model family, dataset or sampling. `target_leakage` fires on **zero** of twelve leaked-feature case-model combinations across both datasets, including Olist's random forest, confirming the miss is not an artefact of which model families are tested. Inspecting the gate's own saved leakage output explains why: the model's own measured "power" reaches 1.0 on DataCo, yet the check reports "no single feature approaches the model's own predictive power" — it tests each feature's *solo* predictive power against the whole model's, and this leak becomes informative only in combination with an existing feature (the scheduled delivery window) already in the model (Section 6.5).

Exact-duplicate detection is where the stock check's own named mechanism and the aggregate verdict diverge sharply, and the divergence itself differs by dataset: DataCo's `split_overlap` never fires at this battery's own 20% severity (0/3 models); Olist's fires on all 3. Section 5.3 traces this to a quantified, dataset-specific sampling effect. The group-overlap defect has no corresponding stock-gate check at all (marked n/a in Figure 1) — this is the structural finding Section 5.4 and 5.5 examine.

## 5.3 Integration effects: the same fitted model, capped vs. uncapped

An earlier version of this comparison pulled the capped row from a separately generated battery CSV that could, and did, go stale the moment that file was regenerated by a later, corrected run — the capped row then silently came from a different population/splitter version than the uncapped row it was being compared against (confirmed directly: the stale capped figure recorded 138,094 DataCo training rows against the current pipeline's 137,492). This section's capped and uncapped checks are instead run against the **identical fitted model and prediction vector** in a single pass: fit once, score once, then run the gate twice — once on the full index arrays, once on a subsample built the same way the gate caps its own input (`_cap`, seed 0).

| Defect | Full-data overlap measured | Full-data check status | Capped (4,000/side) overlap measured | Capped check status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DataCo exact duplicate | 20.00% (6,576/32,880 injected; 0 coincidental) | BLOCKED | 0.47% (19/4,000) | OK — 42.6x understated |
| Olist exact duplicate | 20.04% (3,854/19,236; 3,847 injected, 7 coincidental) | BLOCKED | 1.15% (46/4,000) | SPLIT_OVERLAP_RISK — 17.4x understated |
| DataCo group/order overlap | 0.00% | OK | 0.00% | OK |
| Olist group/customer overlap | 0.00% (feature-row comparison) | OK | 0.00% | OK |

Confirmed via exact injected-row ground truth rather than the hash count alone: DataCo's full-data recovery is exact (6,576 injected, 6,576 hash matches, zero coincidental); Olist's full-data hash count (3,854) includes 7 rows that coincidentally collide on its narrower, lower-cardinality feature set without being injected duplicates at all. The understatement factor is dataset-specific — 42.6-fold on DataCo, 17.4-fold on Olist — because DataCo's 4,000-row cap represents a smaller share of its larger full test set (12.2%) than Olist's does of its smaller one (20.8%), so the same proportional information loss lands on different sides of the 1% threshold: Olist's capped measurement crosses it, DataCo's does not, at this battery's own 20% severity.

![Figure 2. The stock gate's duplication check against its own sampling cap.](C:/Users/pingc/OneDrive/Documents/BDP-Validation-Paper/results/figures/fig2_sampling_sensitivity.png)

Figure 2's left panel extends this to the severity/seed sweep (Section 4.7): the capped check's own measured overlap rises with severity on both datasets, but DataCo's mean never crosses the 1% NEEDS_REVIEW threshold even at 30% injected duplication, while Olist's crosses it between 10% and 20%. This is an integration effect, not a defect in the check's logic: the same check, shown the full data, recovers the true rate on both datasets (right panel).

Group/order overlap: the stock gate's row-level `split_overlap` check detects **zero** percent of this defect on full, uncapped data, on both datasets. This is not a sampling artefact — it does not improve with more data, because the rows this defect moves into training are different, distinct records that do not share an identical or near-identical feature vector with the rows still being scored.

## 5.4 Extension capability, part one: the extension and the temporal check both close the gap, in this battery

The group-identifier extension and the temporal-integrity check both flag every instance of the group-overlap and exact-duplicate defects in this battery (Figure 1, Figure 3). Comparing the stock gate's verdict against the extended gate's verdict row by row, the verdict category changes in exactly 5 of 42 rows — all the two row-comparison-dependent defects, where the stock gate alone stayed at NEEDS_REVIEW.

![Figure 3. Verdict transitions: stock gate vs. extended gate.](C:/Users/pingc/OneDrive/Documents/BDP-Validation-Paper/results/figures/fig3_verdict_transition.png)

Olist's group-overlap defect is the one case where the extension and the temporal check disagree at the aggregate-verdict level: the extension's own measured overlap (1.13%, time-aware, excluding legitimate past-customer history) sits above its 1% review threshold but below its 5% block threshold, so two of three Olist models stay at NEEDS_REVIEW under the extended gate; the temporal check, which has no magnitude threshold, BLOCKS all three outright. This is not a flaw in either check — the extension is deliberately graduated by magnitude; the temporal check reports a binary fact with nothing to graduate.

## 5.5 Extension capability, part two: this battery cannot separate the two mechanisms, and a direct corruption test shows why that matters

By construction (Section 4.3), every row either defect moves into training carries a decision time at or after the held-out window's start — the defects are built by moving genuinely-overlapping-in-time records into the wrong partition, which is simultaneously a group-identity violation and a temporal-ordering violation. No case in this battery isolates one from the other. Given that, this paper's evidence does **not** establish that the group-identifier extension detects anything in this specific battery beyond what the free temporal check already catches.

The timestamp-corruption test (Section 4.7) makes concrete what each check's value actually depends on:

| Dataset | Policy | Extension, real timestamps | Extension, corrupted timestamps | Temporal check, real timestamps | Temporal check, corrupted timestamps |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DataCo | order id, not time-aware | BLOCKED (79.6% overlap) | BLOCKED (79.6%, unchanged) | BLOCKED | PASS |
| Olist | customer id, time-aware | NEEDS_REVIEW (1.1% overlap) | PASS (0.0% overlap) | BLOCKED | PASS |

DataCo's extension is genuinely resilient to a corrupted training-timestamp column, because its declared policy never uses time as an input at all. Olist's extension is **not** resilient — its time-awareness is exactly what makes it correctly ignore a customer's genuinely past orders, and a corrupted timestamp exploits the identical mechanism, fooling it into treating the corrupted (fake-past) rows as legitimate history. The temporal-integrity check is fooled by the same corruption on both datasets, for the same reason. An earlier draft of this paper claimed the extension's advantage over the temporal check was general resilience to timestamp corruption; this test shows that claim holds only for DataCo's non-time-aware policy. The extension's demonstrated, battery-independent value that survives this test is narrower: a named, attributable diagnostic (which group, what fraction), and, specifically for a non-time-aware policy like DataCo's order-id check, continued function when the timestamp column cannot be trusted — not a general property of "the extension" as a class.

## 5.6 Integration effects, continued: false positives on supplementary periods, and what corroborates them

The supplementary-period sweep (eight rolling-origin windows per dataset not used by either scenario case, sixteen total) shows the stock and extended gate flagging (NEEDS_REVIEW or BLOCKED) 100% of DataCo's eight periods and 7 of 8 (87.5%) of Olist's, against 0% and 50% for the automated checklist and 0% for the temporal check on both. Splitting these flags by whether the same-period metrics-only rule also flags the same period (Section 4.8's corroboration lens, not independent validation) shows the two datasets tell different stories: **none** of DataCo's stock/extended-gate flags on these eight periods are metrics-corroborated — the metrics-only rule itself never flags any of them — while on Olist, 4 of 7 flagged periods (57%) are metrics-corroborated and 3 (43%) are methodology-specific. Figure 4 extends this same decomposition to the main battery's clean reference and both scenario cases.

![Figure 4. Flags on legitimate (non-defect) cases: how much is corroborated?](C:/Users/pingc/OneDrive/Documents/BDP-Validation-Paper/results/figures/fig4_review_burden_corroboration.png)

The stock and extended gate flag 100% of these main-battery non-defect cases, of which 61% is metrics-corroborated and 39% is methodology-specific (driven overwhelmingly by feature-drift checks reacting to real but performance-neutral distributional differences, confirmed directly in the gate's own saved check output). The temporal-integrity check flags 0% of every legitimate case tested in this paper, main battery and supplementary periods alike — the cleanest precision result in this study.

## 5.7 Decision usefulness: does a flag predict a genuinely subsequent period's own outcome?

Figure 5 reports the subsequent-period check (Section 4.7): across 18 origin-pairs pooled over both datasets, whether a flag on origin *i* predicts origin *i+1*'s own, independently fitted model failing the common 0.65 AUROC acceptance threshold.

![Figure 5. Does a flag predict the next period's own acceptance outcome?](C:/Users/pingc/OneDrive/Documents/BDP-Validation-Paper/results/figures/fig5_subsequent_period_validity.png)

| Approach | Flagged (of 18) | Precision | Recall |
|---|---|---|---|
| AUROC-only | 5 | 0.80 | 0.80 |
| Metrics-only | 6 | 0.67 | 0.80 |
| Automated checklist | 6 | 0.67 | 0.80 |
| Stock gate | 17 | 0.24 | 0.80 |
| Extended gate | 17 | 0.24 | 0.80 |
| Temporal integrity | 0 | n/a | 0.00 |

The stock and extended gate match the best recall in this table (0.80, tied with every approach except temporal integrity) but achieve it by flagging 17 of 18 origin-pairs — essentially everything — which collapses their precision to 0.24, roughly a third of AUROC-only's (0.80) and just over a third of metrics-only's and the checklist's (0.67 each). A reviewer using the stock or extended gate's flag as a release-readiness signal would, on this evidence, be right about as often as chance on whether the *next* release is actually the one to worry about. The temporal-integrity check, which targets group and temporal dependence rather than generic performance drift, correctly detects none of these origin-pairs' acceptance failures — not a comparable failure mode, and not evidence against the check, but a reminder that no single approach in this battery is a complete release-readiness signal on its own. This small-sample result (9 origin-pairs per dataset) is reported descriptively, not as a significance test (Section 7).

# 6. Discussion

## 6.1 The four-way distinction, applied to a prospective adopter's actual decision

A firm deciding whether to adopt this tool is not asking "is it good." It is asking four narrower, separately answerable questions this paper's structure mirrors directly. Does the stock library's own logic detect what it claims to (Section 5.2)? Given that logic, does our specific integration — a row cap chosen for tractability, in this case — preserve or degrade it (Section 5.3)? If we extend the tool, does the extension add anything a cheaper alternative does not (Sections 5.4-5.5)? And, ultimately, does any flag the tool produces predict a real future problem rather than only reacting to the same period's own numbers (Section 5.7)? This paper's answer to each is specific and bounded, not a single verdict on "the library."

## 6.2 Stock capability is bounded to checks a cheap checklist already matches

The stock gate's named checks add value nowhere the automated checklist does not already match it on this battery: schema enforcement is matched exactly (6/6 both); duplication detection, where it works at all, depends on the same row-hash logic the checklist computes directly on full data rather than a capped sample. On the one defect type with no corresponding stock check at all (group overlap), the checklist also detects nothing, and the gate needs an extension to detect anything (Section 6.3). The one defect no automated approach in this battery catches is the combination leak (Section 6.5). The honest summary is narrower than "the gate catches more than a checklist": on this evidence, it does not, except where an extension is added, and even there the extension's advantage over a free alternative is not established by this battery (Section 6.3).

## 6.3 An integration choice, not a library defect, degraded duplication detection — and the degradation is dataset-specific, not generic

The 4,000-row cap genuinely degrades the duplication check's sensitivity, now measured under a same-fit comparison that removes any doubt about whether the capped and uncapped numbers describe the same model: 42.6-fold on DataCo, 17.4-fold on Olist. Whether this crosses the check's own blocking threshold is not a fixed property of the defect — it depends on how large a share of the full test set the cap represents, which differs by dataset. A firm using this gate should never row-cap a check whose logic depends on comparing individual training rows against test rows without separately testing, on data resembling its own evaluation scale, whether the cap preserves the check's stated sensitivity; "it worked on dataset X" is not evidence it will work on dataset Y at the same cap.

## 6.4 The extension and the temporal check: a narrower, more honest claim than an earlier draft made

RQ3's extended check was built to test a specific claim — that a check operating on a declared group identifier recovers the overlap defects the stock check's row-level comparison structurally cannot see — and Section 5.4 confirms it does, on both datasets, with no false positives on any clean or scenario case. The same section shows a trivial, identifier-free temporal check recovers the identical defects, at the identical rate, at a fraction of the cost, with a lower review burden, and with zero false positives across every supplementary legitimate period tested, where the stock and extended gate flagged nearly all of them. Because this battery's defect-injection mechanism makes every group-identity violation simultaneously a temporal-ordering violation, this paper cannot and does not claim the extension detects anything beyond what the temporal check already provides *in this battery*. The timestamp-corruption test (Section 5.5) adds a further, dataset-specific qualification an earlier draft's general "resilience to timestamp corruption" claim did not have: that advantage exists only for a non-time-aware policy (DataCo's), not for a time-aware one (Olist's), which is exactly as vulnerable to a corrupted timestamp as the temporal check it is being compared against. A firm choosing between the two should, on this evidence, start with the one-line temporal check, add the fuller extension only where diagnostic attribution matters or where the group policy can be built without relying on a timestamp at all, and should not expect a time-aware group policy to be a hedge against a corrupted clock.

## 6.5 Where the leaked-feature miss sits in the leakage taxonomy, and why that placement predicts it will recur

Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) distinguish leakage from a single feature directly encoding the target from leakage from non-independence between training and test samples. The combination leak this paper constructed sits in neither category cleanly: no single column, inspected alone, gives away the answer, and no row is shared or duplicated between training and test. It is a third pattern — a feature that is individually innocuous but, combined with an existing feature already in the model, reconstructs a near-deterministic relationship the model was never meant to access. A single-feature-versus-whole-model test, of the kind the stock gate runs, is well matched to the first category and poorly matched to this third one by construction: detecting it would require testing feature *subsets* against the whole model's power, a combinatorially larger check than the one the gate currently runs. This paper tested this specific combination, at this specific severity, on three model families on two datasets; it did not vary the leak's construction, so the claim supported is that this check misses this specific, realistic combination leak under the conditions tested, not that it would miss every possible combination leak at every severity.

## 6.6 Decision usefulness is the question a same-period comparator cannot answer, and the one this battery's aggregate verdict fails

RQ4's subsequent-period check is, on reflection, the most consequential single result in this paper, because it tests the question a release process actually cares about: not "did something get flagged," but "did the flag mean anything about what happens next." The stock and extended gate's precision collapse to roughly a third of the cheaper alternatives' at matched recall is not explained by either dataset in isolation — DataCo never fails the acceptance criterion at any origin in this series, so every DataCo flag from the stock/extended gate is, by construction, a false positive with respect to this specific check; Olist fails at 4 of 9 usable origin-pairs, giving the gate's flags more genuine signal to be right about, which it still mostly is not. A firm relying on the aggregate gate verdict as a proxy for "will the next release also be a problem" is, on this evidence, better served by a cheaper rule that flags less often but is right more often when it does.

# 7. Limitations

The defects injected here are deliberately clean, single-mechanism constructions; Andrews, Briand and Labiche (2005) found artificially seeded faults can behave differently from the real faults they approximate. This round added a severity/seed sweep for the one defect with a free severity parameter and eight supplementary legitimate periods per dataset, which materially strengthens the evidence that this battery's central findings are not an artefact of one arbitrarily chosen construction — but the leaked-feature, invalid-schema and group-overlap defects remain single, fixed constructions at one severity under one seed. This paper's central finding about the group-overlap defect — that a trivial temporal check matches the extension's detection — is bounded by a specific, disclosed property of this battery's own construction: every defect instance moves a test-period row into training, so no case separates a group-identity violation that respects temporal order from one that does not, and this paper explicitly does not know whether such a case would still be caught by the temporal check alone; a battery purpose-built to decouple the two conditions is the direct next step this paper's own evidence motivates. The subsequent-period check (Section 5.7) rests on 9 origin-pairs per dataset, 18 pooled — far too few for a formal significance test, reported descriptively, and specific to the 0.65 AUROC acceptance threshold used throughout this paper rather than any threshold a real deployment might set. The frozen thresholds in Section 4.4 are a single choice among many defensible ones; Section 5.4 already shows the extended check's own threshold is the specific reason Olist's group-overlap defect does not cross into BLOCKED under that approach, so threshold sensitivity is not a minor caveat but a result-shaping choice reported transparently rather than tuned away. Both datasets concern late-delivery prediction specifically; whether this battery's findings generalise to other supply chain prediction tasks or other governance tools is a question this design cannot answer. The two non-gate comparators were authored by the same team that built the gate integration being compared against them. The label-availability correction (Section 4.2) addresses one specific, disclosed form of leakage in the reference population's own construction; it does not address every way a retrospective, delivered-only population (Olist) or a simulated dataset (DataCo) might still not represent the population a live system would actually score, and DataCo's day-granularity approximation of label-resolution time is a property of the source data this paper's correction cannot improve on. Finally, this paper tests one version of one tool (BDP Model Gate v0.6.0) under one configuration; its findings are evidence about that tool's current default behaviour and this paper's specific extensions, not a general claim about automated governance tooling as a category.

# 8. Conclusion

Separating stock capability, integration effects, extension capability and decision usefulness — rather than reporting one aggregate verdict — produces a materially more specific and more defensible account than this battery's earlier drafts gave. The stock BDP Model Gate reliably catches a schema mismatch no performance-only review can see at all, matched exactly by a much cheaper checklist; its duplication check's sensitivity depends on a sampling-cap choice this paper's own application made, not on anything inherent to the defect, and the degradation is dataset-specific rather than a fixed discount; it misses a feature-combination leak under every condition tested; and its own row-level comparison cannot see a genuine, distinct-record group-overlap defect at any sample size, for a structural reason — but a one-line check of whether any training row's timestamp falls inside the scored window catches the same two defects at a fraction of the gate's cost, with a materially lower review burden, and with zero false positives across every supplementary legitimate period tested, where the stock and extended gate flagged nearly all of them. Measured against the standard that should matter most to an adopting firm — does a flag predict a genuinely subsequent period's own outcome, not merely agree with a same-period rule the study's own team wrote — the stock and extended gate are markedly the worst of six approaches tested, trading recall it shares with every other comparator for a precision collapse driven by flagging almost everything. The practical guidance this paper offers is correspondingly specific: trust the schema check once tested at your own evaluation scale; never row-cap a row-comparison check without separately testing whether the cap preserves its sensitivity on data like yours; add a one-line temporal check before building a fuller group-identity extension, since this evidence does not show the latter catching anything the former does not; do not expect a time-aware group policy to be more robust to a corrupted timestamp than the check it is meant to improve on; and evaluate any release-review tool's flags against what actually happens next, not only against whether its own rules agree with each other on the same data.
