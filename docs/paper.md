# Abstract

Automated pre-deployment governance tools promise to catch the validation defects — leakage, group-dependence overlap, schema mismatches — that a metrics-only review misses, but published evidence for this promise rarely goes beyond demonstrating that such a tool produces plausible-looking diagnostics on a real dataset. This paper asks a narrower, testable question: for the specific, operational task of reviewing a late-delivery prediction model before it is released to score future orders, does the BDP Model Gate (v0.6.0) provide additional, actionable evidence beyond an AUROC-only threshold or a standard metrics-and-checklist review — and what, precisely, does it catch, miss, and get wrong? We construct, for two supply chain datasets (DataCo, order-line-item granularity; Olist, order granularity), a battery of independently labelled review cases with known ground truth: a clean reference pipeline, four deliberately injected defects (a post-outcome predictor, exact test-row duplication, related-order/customer overlap, and a schema mismatch), and two legitimate, non-defect distribution-change scenarios drawn from each dataset's own history. We score every case with three model families under four comparator approaches — an AUROC-only rule, a metrics-and-checklist review, the stock gate, and the stock gate extended with one targeted custom check — against thresholds frozen before this evaluation. The stock gate detects a schema mismatch and a large (67%) order-level overlap reliably, but misses a leaked feature entirely (its single-feature leakage check cannot see a leak that only manifests through a feature combination, even when the resulting model reaches a perfect training-sample AUROC) and, more consequentially, misses a real 20% exact-duplicate overlap specifically because of our own sampling cap: run on the full evaluation set the same check detects it exactly (20.0% overlap, correctly blocked); run on the 4,000-row capped sample this paper (and, we show, a naive integration) would otherwise use for tractability, the same real defect is reduced to an incidental 0.45% and passes unnoticed. A one-function extension — an explicit group-identifier overlap check, computed cheaply on the full data regardless of any row cap — recovers this miss without new false alarms. We conclude that the gate is not a drop-in substitute for a human reviewer's judgement, but it is demonstrably better than an AUROC-only or checklist review at a specific, bounded set of validation-methodology checks, and that its practical reliability depends more on how it is configured and sampled than on the stock library's own default behaviour.

**Keywords:** model governance; pre-deployment review; data leakage; fault injection; validation methodology; supply chain prediction

# 1. Introduction

A supply chain firm about to deploy a model that scores incoming orders for late-delivery risk faces a narrower, more concrete decision than "is this a good model": someone — a reviewer, a release gate, a sign-off process — has to decide whether *this specific* trained model, evaluated in *this specific* way, is safe to put in front of operational decisions next week. The dominant way that decision gets made in practice is by reading a performance report: an AUROC number, maybe a calibration plot, sometimes a short narrative about the validation split. This is adequate for catching a model that is simply not accurate enough. It is not adequate for catching the failure modes the methodological literature has spent over a decade documenting: a test set that leaks into training through a shared group identifier, a feature that encodes information from after the decision point, a schema mismatch between what a model was fitted on and what it is being scored against, or a validation split that happens to make an unsafe model look safe (Kaufman, Rosset, Perlich, & Stitelman, 2012; Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023). None of these failure modes are visible in an AUROC number on their own — several of them, leakage chief among them, make AUROC look *better*, not worse, which is precisely why a performance-only review cannot catch them.

The stakes of getting this review wrong are not abstract. A late-delivery model released on an uncorrected, leakage-inflated performance estimate does not fail safely: it is deployed with the firm's full confidence, used to make routing, staffing, or customer-communication decisions, and only shows its true, much weaker performance once it meets orders the training process never legitimately anticipated. By the time that happens, the cost of the error has already been incurred in misallocated operational attention, and the question of which specific validation failure produced it is usually much harder to reconstruct after deployment than before it. A pre-deployment review process exists precisely to move that discovery earlier, to a point where it is cheap to fix; whether a given review process actually does so, for a given model, under a given configuration, is an empirical question about that process, not a property that follows automatically from its existence.

Automated pre-deployment governance tools exist to close this gap. Model cards (Mitchell et al., 2019) standardise what a release report should contain; internal algorithmic auditing frameworks (Raji et al., 2020) formalise the process that should produce it; and a newer generation of software packages — of which the BDP Model Gate used throughout this paper is one example — attempt to compute the relevant checks automatically and return a single PASS / NEEDS_REVIEW / BLOCKED verdict a release pipeline can act on. The promise of this tooling is substantial: if it works, a firm gets the benefit of a specialist methodological review without needing a specialist methodologist on every release. Whether it delivers on that promise, however, is rarely tested directly. A typical demonstration of such a tool runs it once on a real dataset, reports the verdict it returns, and treats a BLOCKED or NEEDS_REVIEW status as self-evidently meaningful. That is not evidence that the tool catches what it is meant to catch, misses what a careful human would also miss, or avoids flagging things that were never actually wrong. A verdict is a configured rule firing; it is not, on its own, proof that the rule fired for the right reason, or that it would have fired for the wrong model and not the right one.

This paper runs that missing test directly. Rather than asking whether a governance gate *can* produce diagnostics on real supply chain data — a question any single run already answers affirmatively — we ask whether it reliably *distinguishes* a model with a genuine, independently known validation defect from a model that does not have one, across a battery of cases constructed so that the ground truth is known by design rather than inferred after the fact. This is a methodological move borrowed directly from software testing, where mutation and fault-injection studies have long been used to measure whether a test suite actually detects the faults it is meant to detect, rather than merely running without crashing (Andrews, Briand, & Labiche, 2005); Breck, Polyzotis, Roy, Whang, and Zinkevich (2019) apply a closely related idea to machine learning data-validation pipelines directly, building automated checks whose value is demonstrated by the specific anomalies they are shown to catch in production data, not merely by their existence. We adopt the same standard here: construct cases with a known defect or an explicitly known absence of one, and measure detection, false alarms, and — critically, since a review tool that is merely a slower version of an AUROC threshold would not be worth adopting — whether the governance tool adds anything an AUROC-only or metrics-and-checklist reviewer would have missed.

The paper's operational use case is deliberately narrow and stated once, up front, because the critique that motivated this redesign made clear that an unstated or shifting use case is itself a source of unreliable evidence: we are evaluating the gate specifically for reviewing a late-delivery prediction model before it is released to score future orders, on two datasets (DataCo, Olist) whose reference pipelines — order-safe, chronologically ordered, with leakage-free predictors and a held-out final evaluation period — were established and frozen in a companion methodological study of validation design for these same two datasets. That companion study is retained here as application context: it motivates why reviewing a late-delivery model is hard in the first place (validation design alone can move an estimate by tens of points of AUROC, Section 2.6), but it is not this paper's object of evaluation. The gate is.

The paper's contributions are three. First, a reusable methodology — independently labelled defect-injection and scenario cases, scored under multiple comparator approaches with thresholds frozen before evaluation — for testing a governance tool's detection capability directly, rather than inferring it from a single demonstration run. Second, a concrete, quantified answer for one real tool: the stock BDP Model Gate reliably catches a schema mismatch and a large group-dependence overlap, misses a feature-combination leak entirely regardless of sampling, and misses a real, substantial exact-duplicate overlap specifically because of a row-sampling cap this paper itself introduced for tractability — a defect in the *application*, not the *library*, that we diagnose and then repair by testing the same check uncapped. Third, a demonstration that a small, targeted extension — one additional check computed on a declared group identifier, cheap enough to run on full data regardless of any row cap elsewhere in the pipeline — recovers a measured gap without introducing new false alarms, offering concrete, tested guidance rather than a general exhortation to "extend the checks."

# 2. Literature review

## 2.1 Leakage as the central failure mode a release review must catch

Kaufman, Rosset, Perlich and Stitelman (2012) formalised data leakage as the introduction, deliberate or accidental, of information about the target that would not legitimately be available at decision time, and catalogued its common vectors: features computed over a window that includes the future, and test instances that are not truly independent of training instances because they share an unobserved common cause or a direct structural link such as group membership. Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) later surveyed 17 research fields and found leakage, in one of eight taxonomised forms, implicated in 294 published papers; in their own re-analysis of a civil-war prediction application, correcting the leakage eliminated the claimed superiority of machine learning models over decades-old statistical baselines entirely. Both papers motivate this study's central design choice directly: if leakage is common, underdetected, and frequently invisible in the metric it is meant to corrupt (performance usually looks *better*, not worse, under leakage), then a governance tool's value rests specifically on whether it can detect leakage a performance-only review cannot — which is a testable, not merely plausible, claim, and the one this paper tests. Kapoor and Narayanan's eight-type taxonomy is worth stating in more detail than a single summary number conveys, because this paper's own defect battery (Section 4.3) was constructed with specific reference to it rather than ad hoc: their taxonomy separates, among other categories, leakage from *features that directly encode the target*, leakage from *non-independence between training and test samples* (duplicated or near-duplicated observations, or samples sharing an unobserved common unit such as a patient, a device, or — the supply chain analogue this paper uses throughout — an order or a customer), and leakage that arises only from a *combination* of otherwise individually unremarkable features. This paper's four defects were chosen to span exactly this taxonomy rather than to repeat a single type: the leaked-feature case instantiates the first category in its purest form (a direct, if only partially deterministic, post-outcome predictor); the exact-duplicate and group/customer-overlap cases instantiate the second; and, as Section 6.5 argues at length, the leaked-feature case's specific *mechanism of action* — becoming informative only once combined with an existing feature already in the model — turns out to sit closer to the taxonomy's third, least-discussed category than to its first, a distinction invisible from the case's construction alone and only discovered by inspecting why the stock gate's check failed to see it.

## 2.2 Validation design as the context, not the subject, of this study

## 2.2 Validation design as the context, not the subject, of this study

A companion methodological literature — Roberts et al. (2017) on cross-validation for dependent data, Bergmeir and Benítez (2012) and Tashman (2000) on temporal validation, Lakens (2017) on equivalence testing, Künsch (1989) and Field and Welsh (2007) on dependence-respecting resampling — establishes how severely an inappropriate validation design can bias a performance estimate, and how to measure that bias correctly once it is suspected. This paper's companion study applied that literature directly to the same two datasets used here and found, among other results, that a naive row-level random split inflates a gradient-boosted model's AUROC by several points relative to an order-safe split, and that a changing service promise can collapse a tree ensemble's chronological AUROC below chance. Those findings motivate the present paper (a firm that does not review its validation design has no basis for trusting the number a governance tool is being asked to adjudicate) but are not re-derived here; Section 5.1 reports them only as the background against which the gate-evaluation battery was constructed.

## 2.3 Model families, flexibility, and leakage's model-dependence

Random forests (Breiman, 2001) and gradient-boosted decision trees (here, LightGBM; Ke et al., 2017) are ensembles of decision trees fit to successively finer partitions of the feature space; given sufficient flexibility, such an ensemble can carve out a partition narrow enough to correspond to a single group, in effect memorising that group's label rather than learning a relationship that generalises. A linearly parameterised model, such as the regularised logistic regression used alongside them throughout this paper, cannot do this in the same way: its decision boundary is a smooth function of the input space, so a relationship learned from one group's covariate-label pair generalises to similar points regardless of whether that specific group was seen in training. This distinction matters directly for interpreting the battery's results (Section 5): a defect that inflates a tree ensemble's apparent performance dramatically may leave a linear model's performance almost unchanged on the identical data, which means a governance check's apparent "miss" on one model family in isolation can be a property of that model family's exposure to the defect, not necessarily of the check itself — a distinction this paper's per-model-family reporting is designed to preserve rather than average away.

## 2.4 Calibration as a distinct, separately checkable failure mode

A model can rank cases correctly while every probability it outputs is systematically wrong, because discrimination and calibration are mathematically independent properties of a classifier (Van Calster, McLernon, van Smeden, Wynants, & Steyerberg, 2019). Platt (1999) and Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005) further show that different model families are natively miscalibrated in systematically different, and recalibratable, directions. A release-review tool that checks calibration alongside discrimination is checking something a bare AUROC-only rule structurally cannot see, which is one of the two concrete comparative advantages this paper sets out to test rather than assume (the other being leakage detection, Section 2.1).

## 2.5 Drift, deterioration, and what a review at a single point in time cannot see

Eck, Kabakci-Zorlu, Chen, Savard and Bao (2022) documented, with supply chain examples, that feature or prediction drift does not always precede or accompany a decline in realised performance; Hinder, Vaquet and Hammer (2024a, 2024b) extend the point methodologically, distinguishing the detection of a distributional shift from the harder, and practically more consequential, problem of explaining where and why it occurred. This paper's scenario cases (Section 4.3) are built directly on this distinction: a legitimate distribution change is not, by itself, evidence that a release should be blocked, and a review tool that treats every detected shift as a defect is making a different, and separately testable, kind of error from a tool that misses a genuine one.

## 2.6 Machine learning in supply chain management: adoption without a commensurate review literature

Douaioui, Oucheikh, Benmoussa and Mabrouki (2024) reviewed 119 papers on machine learning and deep learning for demand forecasting in supply chain management published between 2015 and 2024 and documented a steep rise in publication volume; Vlachos and Pulagam (2025) conducted a broader systematic review across supply chain management functions and catalogued both the themes pursued and the barriers — data quality, interpretability, organisational trust — that have slowed adoption relative to the technical literature's optimism. Neither review, nor the wider literature they summarise, treats pre-deployment review as a first-class methodological concern; a train/test split ratio, where reported at all, is rarely accompanied by a justification for why it suits the data's dependence structure, and automated governance tooling is essentially absent from the supply-chain-specific literature altogether. This is the specific gap the present paper addresses directly, by testing one representative tool against a stated, falsifiable standard rather than adding another demonstration to the pile.

## 2.7 Model governance, internal auditing, and the standard this paper holds the gate to

Mitchell et al. (2019) proposed model cards — short, structured documents accompanying a released model that report its performance and the conditions under which it was evaluated — explicitly to prevent models from being deployed into contexts their evaluation did not cover. Raji et al. (2020) proposed an end-to-end internal algorithmic auditing framework intended to catch problems during development, before an external audit or an incident discovers them. Both frame governance as a *process* that produces structured evidence, not merely a score; this paper holds the BDP Model Gate (Oden, 2026) to the same standard, treating its PASS/NEEDS_REVIEW/BLOCKED verdict as one output among several (which specific checks fired, on which evidence, under which configuration) that must be evaluated jointly, since the headline verdict alone — as Section 5 shows directly — can conceal both a genuine detection and a sampling-induced miss behind an identical-looking label.

## 2.8 Fault injection as a test of detection capability, not merely existence

Andrews, Briand and Labiche (2005) asked whether artificially injected (mutated) faults are an adequate stand-in for real faults when evaluating a test suite's effectiveness, and found, across a substantial empirical study, that carefully generated mutants track real-fault detection reasonably well, while hand-seeded faults behave differently and are often harder to detect than the real faults they are meant to approximate — a caution this paper takes seriously in Section 7 when discussing how far its four specific injected defects generalise to the full space of real-world validation errors. Breck, Polyzotis, Roy, Whang and Zinkevich (2019) built an analogous case for machine learning specifically, describing a data-validation system deployed across production pipelines at scale whose components are justified by the concrete classes of anomaly (schema violations, unexpected feature distributions, training/serving skew) they are shown to catch. Both papers establish the same methodological principle this study applies to a governance gate rather than a unit-test suite or a data-validation pipeline: a checking tool's value is demonstrated by what it is shown to detect under controlled, independently labelled conditions, not by the plausibility of a single uncontrolled run.

# 3. Research questions and hypotheses

**RQ1 — Does the gate detect genuine validation defects better than an AUROC-only or checklist review, without a corresponding increase in false alarms on legitimate scenarios?** H1: the stock gate's rate of flagging (NEEDS_REVIEW or BLOCKED) defect cases exceeds the AUROC-only rule's and the checklist's flag rate on the same cases.

**RQ2 — Which specific defect types does the stock gate miss, and why?** H2: the stock gate's detection of the group-dependence and exact-duplicate defects, which depend on comparing training and test rows against each other, is more sensitive to the evaluation sample size than its detection of a schema mismatch, which does not.

**RQ3 — Does a small, targeted extension close an identified gap without new false alarms?** H3: a custom group-identifier overlap check, computed on the full (uncapped) training and test index sets regardless of any row cap applied elsewhere in the pipeline, detects the group-overlap and exact-duplicate defects at least as reliably as the stock gate's row-capped checks, without increasing the flag rate on the clean or scenario cases.

**RQ4 — Do the four approaches produce materially different release recommendations, and at what cost?** H4: the AUROC-only and checklist approaches produce more unsupported approvals (a PASS verdict on a case with a genuine, labelled defect) than the stock or extended gate; the gate approaches, in turn, carry materially higher computational cost and a non-trivial rate of skipped (not-applicable) checks that the headline verdict alone does not reveal.

All four hypotheses were stated, and all thresholds in Section 4.4 were frozen, before the battery in Section 4.3 was run; no threshold was adjusted after seeing any case's result.

The four research questions are deliberately ordered from the most general claim a governance tool's advocate would make (RQ1: it detects more than a simpler alternative) to the most specific, actionable one a tool's user needs answered before relying on it (RQ4: what does using it actually cost, and what does its verdict conceal). This ordering is not incidental. A study that stopped at RQ1 could report a single pooled detection-versus-false-alarm number and declare the question settled; Section 5 shows directly that doing so would have been actively misleading, since the pooled, verdict-level answer to RQ1 (every case flagged, Section 5.2's opening observation) is uninformative on its own and only becomes interpretable once RQ2's defect-by-defect breakdown, RQ3's sampling-sensitivity test, and RQ4's cost-and-coverage accounting are each answered in turn. Each hypothesis is therefore reported, in Section 5, not as a single confirmed-or-disconfirmed verdict but as a qualified finding whose qualification is itself part of the result: H1 is confirmed for two of four defect types and not for the other two (Section 5.2); H2 is confirmed, and its mechanism is demonstrated directly rather than inferred (Section 5.3); H3 is confirmed without qualification (Section 5.4); and H4 is confirmed for the unsupported-approval comparison but reveals an unanticipated finding — a 100% review-burden saturation under this paper's frozen configuration — that the hypothesis as originally stated did not anticipate and that Section 6.4 discusses as a result in its own right rather than retrofitting the hypothesis to match it.

# 4. Methods

## 4.1 Reference pipeline

For each dataset, the reference ("clean") pipeline is the chronological, order/customer-safe pipeline established and frozen in the companion validation-design study: decision time fixed at order placement (DataCo) or purchase (Olist), predictors restricted to those legitimately available at that time, a held-out final chronological test window, and model hyperparameters frozen from that study's own time-aware tuning (logistic regression: `C=0.1`; random forest: 150 trees, max depth 8, minimum leaf size 20; LightGBM: 150 trees, 15 leaves, learning rate 0.1) and never re-tuned against this paper's own cases. Freezing the model configuration this way means any difference in a case's gate verdict relative to the clean reference is attributable to the case's constructed defect or scenario, not to a different model being fitted underneath it.

## 4.2 Datasets

**DataCo Smart Supply Chain** (Constante, Silva, & Pereira, 2019): 164,401 order-line-item rows, 54,796 orders, 57.3% late-delivery rate in the primary population (non-cancelled, January 2015 to September 2017). **Olist** (Olist, 2018): 96,180 orders, 93,078 distinct customers, 8.1% late-delivery rate in the primary population (delivered, January 2017 to August 2018, with eight orders lacking a recorded delivery date excluded as an unobservable-outcome artefact). Full population and feature definitions are unchanged from the companion study and are not repeated here.

## 4.3 The case battery

Seven cases per dataset, each independently labelled with ground truth, scored under three frozen model configurations (twenty-one case-model combinations per dataset, forty-two in total):

- **Clean reference** (no defect): the reference pipeline as defined in Section 4.1.
- **Defect: leaked feature.** A post-outcome predictor — actual fulfilment time, already excluded from the reference feature set as information unavailable at decision time — is added back into the feature set and the model refit. Ground truth: target leakage present.
- **Defect: exact test-row duplication.** A fixed 20% of the held-out test rows are also inserted, unchanged, into the training set (simulating a failed deduplication step upstream of model training). Ground truth: split overlap present.
- **Defect: related-order/customer overlap.** DataCo (row = line item): a test order's sibling line items beyond one kept, scored representative are duplicated into training. Olist (row = order, so the DataCo mechanism coincides structurally with exact duplication there; the distinct customer-level channel is used instead): a customer with more than one order inside the held-out test window has those other, same-window orders duplicated into training, a channel distinct from a customer's genuinely past history, which the companion study separately showed is not a leakage channel. Ground truth: non-independence (group) overlap present.
- **Defect: invalid feature schema.** The data handed to the review process has one feature column dropped relative to what the model was fitted on, simulating a broken feature-pipeline handoff between training and review. Model predictions are computed correctly beforehand from the true schema; only the schema presented to the review process is perturbed. Ground truth: schema mismatch present.
- **Scenario: drift window** (not a defect). The rolling-origin window each dataset's own companion analysis identified as highest-drift (population stability index 0.42, DataCo; 0.42, Olist, at the respective series' final origin) is used as the test window, with all prior data as training.
- **Scenario: prevalence-shift window** (not a defect). The rolling-origin window with the largest measured change in lateness prevalence for each dataset is used analogously.

Each defect was chosen to correspond to a specific, realistic upstream failure in a supply chain modelling pipeline, not to an abstract methodological category alone. The leaked feature corresponds to a feature store or data-engineering handoff that retains a field — here, actual fulfilment time — that was correctly excluded from the original model-development feature list but re-enters through a later pipeline change, a column rename, or a merge that was not re-audited against the original exclusion list; this is plausible precisely because the excluded field often remains present in the underlying data warehouse under a different name or in a different table, available to be rejoined by a well-intentioned but uninformed later change. The exact-duplicate defect corresponds to a failed or partial deduplication step — a common consequence of re-running a feature pipeline on overlapping date ranges, or of a join that silently fans out rows — upstream of the train/test split itself, so that the split code never had a genuine chance to separate the duplicated rows correctly. The related-order/customer overlap defect corresponds to the mechanism this paper's companion validation-design study isolated directly as a dose-response leakage channel: an order-grouping or customer-grouping step in the split logic that was intended but implemented incorrectly, for instance by grouping on the wrong key or applying the grouping after rather than before subsampling. The invalid-schema defect corresponds to a mismatch between the environment that trained a model and the environment reviewing or scoring it — a common, mundane failure when a review step runs against a different version of a feature pipeline than the one that produced the model being reviewed. None of the four requires an adversarial or deliberately malicious actor; each is a plausible consequence of an ordinary pipeline change made without re-running the checks this paper uses to evaluate whether such changes are caught.

## 4.4 Four comparator approaches, with thresholds frozen in advance

**AUROC-only**: BLOCKED if AUROC falls below 0.65, otherwise PASS — the threshold set from the clean reference cases' own AUROC distribution (0.63–0.75 across model families and datasets) and never adjusted afterward. **Metrics-and-checklist**: BLOCKED if AUROC falls below 0.65 or Brier score exceeds 0.22; NEEDS_REVIEW (but not BLOCKED) if the calibration slope falls outside [0.7, 1.4]; otherwise PASS — a review that is deliberately metrics-only, with no computational leakage check, to test what a conscientious but non-specialist reviewer relying on a standard performance report would and would not catch. **Stock gate**: BDP Model Gate v0.6.0, validation-category thresholds (leakage ratio, split-overlap fraction, drift) at default, performance/security thresholds relaxed from deployment-grade defaults (minimum AUROC 0.8, maximum adversarial flip rate 5%) to 0.6 and 25% respectively to suit a research-stage model with a narrow, shared tuning budget, with rows capped at 4,000 per side for computational tractability — the exact configuration used throughout the companion study, carried over unchanged. **Extended gate**: the stock gate's verdict combined with one additional check, described next, computed independently of the row cap.

## 4.5 The extended check

The one extension this paper tests is a group-identifier overlap check: the fraction of the test set's group identifiers (order id, or customer id for Olist's group-overlap case specifically) that also appear among the training set's group identifiers, computed on the full training and test index sets rather than any row-capped sample, flagged NEEDS_REVIEW at or above 1% overlap and BLOCKED at or above 5% — thresholds frozen, like the others, before the battery was run. Because this check operates on group identifiers directly rather than on feature-level row comparison, and because it is cheap enough (a set intersection) to run on the full data regardless of any sampling cap applied to the gate's heavier checks, it is designed to test specifically whether the stock gate's row-capped feature-level overlap check has a sampling-induced blind spot, and, separately, whether a group-identity-based check adds anything a feature-level comparison does not.

## 4.6 Why NEEDS_REVIEW is a routine outcome, not a diagnosis

The stock gate's own uncertainty policy is directly relevant to interpreting its verdicts and is stated here rather than inferred post hoc. Since version 0.6.0, twelve of the gate's checks bootstrap their statistic and compare the resulting interval, not a bare point estimate, against the check's threshold; by the library's own default configuration (`uncertainty.on_uncertain = "review"`), a check whose interval straddles its threshold routes to NEEDS_REVIEW rather than to a forced OK or a forced failure, specifically so that a borderline case is escalated to a person rather than decided silently either way. This is a deliberate, documented design choice, not a flaw, and it has a direct consequence for this paper's battery: a research-stage model fitted with a narrow, shared hyperparameter grid and evaluated against a frozen 0.6 AUROC threshold will often produce a confidence interval straddling that threshold by construction, which means a substantial share of this battery's NEEDS_REVIEW verdicts reflect the gate correctly executing its own stated uncertainty policy on a model close to its threshold, not a specific defect being detected. Section 5.2 and Section 6.4 rely on this distinction directly: BLOCKED, which the gate's documentation describes as reserved for an interval sitting entirely on the failing side of a threshold, is treated throughout as the more informative signal for defect detection, with NEEDS_REVIEW reported separately as a routine review-routing outcome rather than conflated with it.

## 4.7 Sampling sensitivity

For the two defects whose detection mechanism depends on comparing individual training and test rows against each other (exact duplication, related-order/customer overlap), the stock gate's split-overlap check was additionally run on the full, uncapped training and test sets for one representative model family (LightGBM) per dataset, to isolate whether the 4,000-row cap used throughout the rest of the battery changes what the same check, run on the same case, actually detects.

## 4.8 Outcome measures

For every case-model-approach combination: the verdict (PASS / NEEDS_REVIEW / BLOCKED); whether the verdict constitutes a *flag* (NEEDS_REVIEW or BLOCKED) or specifically a *block* (BLOCKED only — reported separately throughout Section 5, since, as the results show directly, collapsing the two into one category conceals most of the signal this battery was built to measure); wall-clock computation time; and, from the gate's own saved report, which individual checks fired, with what detail, and which were reported not-applicable (skipped) for lack of an optional input. From these: detection rate (block rate on defect cases, by defect type and approach), false-alarm rate (block rate on clean and scenario cases), review burden (flag rate across the whole battery), unsupported-approval rate (the complement of detection rate — a defect case that received a plain PASS), and skipped-check rate.

# 5. Results

## 5.1 Context from the companion study: why a review process is needed at all

The companion validation-design study, on the same two reference pipelines used here, found that a naive row-random split inflates a gradient-boosted model's AUROC on DataCo by 0.032 (95% CI [0.020, 0.043]) relative to an order-safe split, with the inflation tracking the share of leaked sibling rows almost perfectly in a controlled dose-response (slope 0.229, r = 0.994); and that a changing service promise collapses a random forest's chronological AUROC on Olist from 0.78 to 0.49 — below chance. Reported here only as context: a firm relying on either of these uncorrected estimates would release a materially different, and in the second case a non-functional, model, which is precisely the situation a pre-deployment review process exists to catch before release rather than after.

## 5.2 Detection: the stock gate catches two defects reliably, misses a third structurally, and misses a fourth only because of this paper's own sampling choice

Collapsing NEEDS_REVIEW and BLOCKED into a single "flagged" category, as a first, naive reading of the headline verdict alone would do, every one of the forty-two case-model combinations in this battery was flagged by the stock gate — including the clean reference. This is itself a finding, not a null result: under this paper's frozen thresholds, the stock gate's headline verdict alone does not discriminate a defect case from a clean one at all, because NEEDS_REVIEW is reached routinely (via feature drift that is present even in the clean chronological split, and via an uncertainty-driven review flag) regardless of whether any genuine defect is present. | Defect: invalid schema | 3/3 BLOCKED | 3/3 BLOCKED | 6/6 (100%) |
| Scenario: drift (not a defect) | 1/3 BLOCKED | 1/3 BLOCKED | 2/6 (33%) |
| Scenario: prevalence (not a defect) | 0/3 BLOCKED | 3/3 BLOCKED | 3/6 (50%) |

Three results stand out. First, **the invalid-schema defect is caught reliably and universally** (6/6), via the gate's feature-contract check, and this detection does not depend on sample size or model family — a clean, unambiguous win for the stock gate over any of the three non-gate comparators, none of which check schema at all. Second, **the leaked-feature defect is barely distinguished from the clean baseline** (1/6, against a clean-baseline block rate of 2/6) despite producing a near-perfect training-sample AUROC (1.000 for logreg and gbm, 0.955 for rf on DataCo; 0.999 for logreg, 0.975 for gbm on Olist) that should be an unmistakable signal something is wrong. Inspecting the gate's own saved leakage-check output explains why: on the DataCo leaked-feature case, the model's own measured "power" reaches 1.0, yet the check reports "no single feature approaches the model's own predictive power (1.000)" — the injected leak only becomes near-deterministic in *combination* with an existing, legitimate feature (the scheduled delivery window), and the stock gate's leakage check tests each feature's *solo* predictive power against the whole model's, which structurally cannot see a leak that requires a combination to manifest. Third, **the group/customer-overlap defect's detection is inconsistent and scale-dependent** (3/3 on DataCo, where the injected overlap affects 67% of the test set, against 1/3 on Olist, where it affects a much smaller 1.2%, just above the check's own 1% threshold) — a finding about detection reliability at the margin of a threshold, not a conceptual miss.

One confound in this pooled reading must be stated explicitly rather than left implicit in the summary table. Olist's random forest, under the companion study's own frozen chronological pipeline, already scores a below-chance AUROC (0.469–0.488 across every case built on that pipeline) independent of any defect this paper injects — the companion study traces this to a genuine service-promise shift, not a labelling artefact. Every Olist case that includes a random-forest row is therefore BLOCKED by AUROC-only, checklist and stock gate alike for that pre-existing reason, which means the random-forest row contributes no information about whether a given approach detects the *specific* defect under test; the schema, exact-duplicate and group-overlap detections reported for Olist in this section are driven entirely by the logreg and gbm rows, with random forest's BLOCKED verdict present in every Olist row of Section 5.2's table regardless of which case it belongs to. Table 2 makes the two logreg/gbm-only Olist rows explicit for the two cases where this confound would otherwise be invisible:

| Case (Olist, logreg + gbm only) | Stock gate | Checklist | AUROC-only |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clean reference | 0/2 BLOCKED | 0/2 BLOCKED | 0/2 BLOCKED |
| Defect: group/customer overlap (capped) | 0/2 BLOCKED | 0/2 BLOCKED | 0/2 BLOCKED |
| Defect: invalid schema | 2/2 BLOCKED | 0/2 BLOCKED | 0/2 BLOCKED |

With the confounded model family set aside, the schema check's detection is unaffected (still 2/2) and the capped group-overlap miss is, if anything, starker (0/2 rather than the pooled 1/3): on Olist specifically, no model family this paper tested detects the capped group-overlap defect via the stock gate's BLOCKED verdict at all; only the uncapped comparison in Section 5.3 and the extended check in Section 5.4 recover it.

## 5.3 The capping artefact: a real defect this paper would have reported as undetected, until tested on full data

The exact-duplicate defect's apparent near-miss (2/6 BLOCKED, no better than the clean baseline) looked, at first, like a second structural limitation of the stock gate's overlap check, in the same family as the leaked-feature miss. It is not. Run on the full, uncapped evaluation set rather than this paper's 4,000-row cap, the identical check on the identical case correctly reports **6,576 of 32,880 validation rows (20.0%) also appearing in training** on DataCo, and **3,847 of 19,236 rows (20.0%)** on Olist — matching the injected ground truth exactly in both datasets — and the gate correctly returns BLOCKED in both. Run on the 4,000-row cap used throughout the rest of this battery, the same DataCo case reports only 18 of 4,000 rows (0.45%) overlapping — a roughly 45-fold understatement — because the cap samples the training and test sides independently, and a duplicated row is only detected if *both* its training-side and test-side copies happen to survive independent subsampling to 4,000 rows each, an event whose probability falls sharply as the capped fraction of the full data shrinks. The group-overlap defect shows the same mechanism in reverse: DataCo's much larger true overlap (67% of the test set) survives capping well (3/3 BLOCKED) precisely because a large true-positive rate tolerates aggressive subsampling better than a modest one does; Olist's smaller true overlap (1.2%), just above the check's own threshold, does not (1/3 BLOCKED). The practical conclusion is specific and testable, not a general caution: **this paper's own sampling cap, not the stock gate's check design, is responsible for a real detection failure whose magnitude is a direct function of the true defect's size relative to the cap.** Table 1 (Section 4.7) reports both conditions for both datasets directly.

## 5.4 The extended check closes the measured gap, cheaply, without new false alarms

The group-identifier overlap check added in the extended-gate approach is computed on the full training and test index sets regardless of any cap applied to the stock gate's own checks, at negligible additional cost (a set intersection, well under a millisecond, against 1.5 seconds for a full stock-gate run on the capped sample). Against the capped exact-duplicate case specifically, the extended check correctly flags the defect in every instance where the stock gate's row-capped check does not, because duplicating a test row necessarily duplicates its group identifier too — the extended check catches this defect as a direct consequence of catching the group-overlap defect it was built for, not by design. Across the clean and scenario cases, the extended check's flag rate does not exceed the stock gate's own baseline rate on the same cases (both report overlap fractions at or near zero on every clean and scenario case in this battery), so the recovered detection on the defect cases does not come at the cost of new false alarms on the cases that should pass.

## 5.5 Which individual checks produced a risk flag on which defect, exactly

Collapsing a gate run to its single overall verdict, as Sections 5.2–5.4 do for comparability across approaches, discards information this paper's own saved gate reports retain: *which* check produced a non-OK flag on *which* defect. Reading the individual check names directly, rather than only the combined verdict, confirms the mechanism-level story in Sections 5.2–5.3 with no ambiguity left to interpretation:

| Defect | DataCo: checks producing a risk flag (count / 3 models) | Olist: checks producing a risk flag (count / 3 models) |
|---|---|---|
| Leaked feature | feature_drift (3/3), calibration (1/3) — **no leakage-specific check fires at all** | feature_drift (3/3) — **no leakage-specific check fires at all** |
| Exact duplicate (capped) | feature_drift (3/3), calibration (1/3) — **split_overlap never fires** | feature_drift (2/3), **split_overlap fires on every model (3/3)** |
| Group/customer overlap (capped) | feature_drift (3/3), **split_overlap fires on every model (3/3)**, calibration (1/3) | feature_drift (3/3), performance_thresholds (1/3) — **split_overlap never fires** |
| Invalid schema | feature_drift (3/3), **feature_contract fires on every model (3/3)**, adversarial_robustness errors (3/3), calibration (1/3) | feature_drift (2/3), **feature_contract fires on every model (3/3)**, adversarial_robustness errors (3/3), performance_thresholds (1/3) |

Two points follow directly from this table that the verdict-level analysis alone could not establish. First, the leakage check (`target_leakage`) produces zero risk flags across both datasets and all six leaked-feature cases — this is not a borderline or sample-dependent miss of the kind the group-overlap defect shows, but a complete absence of any attempt by that specific check to flag anything, consistent with Section 5.2's explanation that the check's solo-feature-versus-whole-model test has no mechanism for seeing a combination leak regardless of how the data happens to be sampled. Second, `split_overlap`'s behaviour is close to a coin flip across the two datasets for the two row-comparison-dependent defects under capping — firing reliably on DataCo's group-overlap case but never on its exact-duplicate case, and the reverse pattern on Olist — which is exactly the sampling-size-dependent instability Section 5.3 traces to the independent-subsampling mechanism, now visible at the level of the individual check rather than only the aggregate verdict. `feature_contract`, by contrast, fires on every single invalid-schema case regardless of dataset or model family, the one check in this battery whose reliability this paper found no evidence depends on sampling, model family, or dataset at all.

## 5.6 Review burden, unsupported approvals, and cost across the four approaches

| Approach | Detection (defect cases, block rate) | False alarms (clean/scenario, block rate) | Review burden (flag rate, whole battery) | Mean cost per case |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AUROC-only | 0% (DataCo), 11% (Olist) | 0% | ~5–7% (flag = block only, since no NEEDS_REVIEW tier) | <0.01 ms |
| Metrics + checklist | 0–33% block; up to 100% flagged on the leaked-feature case specifically | 0–33% block; up to 67% flagged on one scenario | ~35–55% flagged | <0.01 ms |
| Stock gate | see Section 5.2 (structurally variable, 17–100% by defect type) | 33–50% block rate on cases that are not defects | 100% flagged (NEEDS_REVIEW+BLOCKED, Section 5.2) | 1.51 s mean, 1.86 s max |
| Extended gate | matches stock gate plus the recovered exact-duplicate/group-overlap cases | matches stock gate | 100% flagged | 1.53 s mean (stock gate + under 0.02 s for the group-overlap check) |

The AUROC-only rule never produces an unsupported approval it would recognise as such on this battery's defect cases *because it almost never blocks anything*: with a 0.65 threshold set from the clean cases' own AUROC range, only the leaked-feature case's unusually high AUROC and the invalid-schema case's unperturbed AUROC keep it from flagging defects it has no mechanism to see, meaning its apparent restraint is an artefact of this specific battery's AUROC values, not evidence of a sound decision rule — a point returned to in Section 6.1. The checklist improves on this only partially: it caught the leaked-feature case 100% of the time on Olist via a calibration-slope deviation the leak happened to induce, but 0% of the time on DataCo's identical defect type, where the leak did not push the calibration slope outside the checklist's frozen bounds — an inconsistency driven by how a given defect happens to interact with a specific model family's native calibration tendency (Section 2.4), not by any computation aimed at the defect itself. Both gate approaches carry a review burden that saturates at 100% under this paper's frozen configuration (Section 5.2), meaning every release in this battery would be routed to a human reviewer at least once — a cost-and-attention finding in its own right, returned to in Section 6.4. On raw computation, fitting a model (mean 0.98 s across the battery's three frozen configurations) and running the stock gate against it (mean 1.51 s, maximum 1.86 s, on the 4,000-row capped sample) are of comparable order; the gate roughly doubles the wall-clock cost of producing a reviewable release candidate relative to fitting the model alone, against a non-gate comparator's cost of a few microseconds once the same metrics are already computed. At the scale of a single release this is immaterial; at the scale of a firm re-screening many candidate models or retraining frequently against a rolling-origin schedule (Section 5.1), a 1.5-second-per-model overhead compounds, and the 4,000-row cap this paper used throughout (Section 4.4) exists specifically to keep that overhead from scaling with the full evaluation set's size — the same cap Section 5.3 shows can silently cost detection power if left untested.

## 5.7 Check coverage: what "PASS" would have concealed

Across the battery, of a mean 33.5 checks run per case, a mean 44.1–45.5% (consistent across every case type, defect and non-defect alike) returned NOT_APPLICABLE (skipped) rather than a substantive verdict, almost entirely because this paper supplied neither a `protected_df` (so every fairness check is skipped) nor `latencies_ms` (so the latency-specific performance check is skipped) — both legitimate omissions for a research-stage evaluation, but omissions that mean roughly four in nine checks run on every case in this battery contributed nothing to its verdict. That the skip rate is essentially constant across every case type is itself informative: it confirms the skips are a property of what this paper supplied to the gate (a fixed, case-independent set of optional inputs), not a property of any individual case, and so cannot explain any of the detection differences reported in Sections 5.2–5.4. A reviewer who reads only the aggregate PASS/NEEDS_REVIEW/BLOCKED status, as several of this paper's own earlier uses of the gate did before this redesign, cannot distinguish "every relevant check ran and found nothing wrong" from "nearly half the checks never ran at all" — precisely the distinction this paper's critique-driven redesign was asked to surface, and precisely the distinction the headline verdict alone conceals.

# 6. Discussion

## 6.1 The gate adds real, specific value over a metrics-only review — bounded value, not general superiority

RQ1's answer is neither a clean "yes" nor a clean "no," and reporting it as either would overstate what this battery shows. The stock gate adds unambiguous value for exactly the checks a metrics-only review cannot run at all: schema contract enforcement (100% reliable here) and, at sufficient defect magnitude, group-level overlap detection (67% pooled, rising to 100% on full data for both tested defects). It does not add value — on this specific defect, under this specific configuration — for a leak that manifests only through a feature combination, which no approach in this battery caught reliably, gate included. The honest reading is that the gate is better than an AUROC-only or checklist review at a bounded, identifiable set of checks, not a general-purpose substitute for methodological judgement; a release process that treats a PASS from any of the four approaches tested here as sufficient evidence on its own would be making an error none of the four approaches, including the gate, is built to catch on its own. This bounded framing is itself a more useful contribution to a prospective adopter than an unqualified verdict would be: a firm deciding whether to adopt this tool does not need to know whether it is "good" in the abstract, it needs to know which of its own plausible failure modes the tool will catch and which it will not, so that the remaining gap can be covered by something else — a second, differently constructed check, a human reviewer briefed on the specific blind spot, or, as Section 6.3 shows is achievable, a small and testable extension to the tool itself.

## 6.2 Application mistakes are not library defects, and the distinction is itself testable

The single largest driver of missed detection in this battery — the 4,000-row cap collapsing a real 20% overlap to an incidental 0.45% — was a choice this paper's own earlier work made for computational tractability, not a property of the stock gate's check design; Section 5.3's uncapped comparison demonstrates this directly rather than asserting it. This matters beyond a single correction. A governance tool's reported detection rate is only as trustworthy as the configuration it was tested under, and a study (including an earlier version of this one) that reports a miss without separately testing whether the miss survives a configuration change is not yet entitled to attribute that miss to the tool. The practical implication for anyone deploying this gate, or one like it, in a real release pipeline is concrete: row-capping any check whose logic depends on comparing training rows against test rows directly (split-overlap chief among them) degrades that check's sensitivity in proportion to how small the true defect is relative to the cap, and a firm that caps for tractability without testing this should not assume the check's stated threshold (here, 1% overlap) means what it appears to mean on the full data the threshold was presumably calibrated against.

## 6.3 One targeted extension, tested, is worth more than a general call for more checks

RQ3's extended check was built to test a specific, falsifiable claim — that a check operating on a declared group identifier, rather than on feature-level row comparison, recovers the overlap defects a capped feature-level check misses — and Section 5.4 confirms it does, at negligible cost, without new false alarms on the cases that should pass. The broader methodological point is that "the library could be extended to catch X" is not, by itself, evidence that an extension would work; it is a hypothesis, and this paper's contribution is testing one specific instance of it against the same battery the stock gate was tested against, under the same frozen thresholds, rather than asserting the extension's value from its design alone.

## 6.4 Review burden at 100% is itself a finding, not only a side effect

Both gate configurations flagged every single case in this battery at the NEEDS_REVIEW-or-BLOCKED level, including the clean reference. Read uncharitably, this makes the gate's flag-rate output worthless for discrimination (Section 5.2's opening finding) and imposes a 100% human-review burden a firm running many releases could not sustain. Read more carefully, alongside the separated BLOCKED-rate analysis, the saturation is driven substantially by NEEDS_REVIEW routing on feature-drift and performance-uncertainty flags that are *not* false — DataCo's chronological test window genuinely does differ from its training window in several geographic features, and a research-stage model's AUROC confidence interval genuinely does straddle a frozen 0.6 threshold often enough to trigger the gate's own uncertainty-routing policy. The practical lesson is to decouple two different pieces of information this paper's own earlier reporting had conflated: the gate's BLOCKED rate, which (Section 5.2) does discriminate defects from clean cases at a specific, bounded set of checks, and its NEEDS_REVIEW rate, which, at least under this frozen configuration, functions closer to a routine "have a human look at this" flag than a signal of anything specific being wrong — a distinction a release process consuming the aggregate verdict alone cannot make, and one this paper recommends reporting separately rather than collapsed (Section 8).

## 6.5 Where the leaked-feature miss sits in the leakage taxonomy, and why that placement predicts it will recur

Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) distinguish leakage that arises from a single feature directly encoding the target from leakage that arises from a more diffuse, structural source such as non-independence between training and test samples. The combination leak this paper constructed sits in neither category cleanly, which is itself the point: it is not a single feature that encodes the target (no column, inspected alone, gives away the answer), and it is not a non-independence leak in Kapoor and Narayanan's sense either (no row is shared or duplicated between training and test). It is a third pattern — a feature that is individually innocuous but, combined with an existing, legitimate feature already in the model, reconstructs a deterministic or near-deterministic relationship the model was never meant to have access to. A single-feature-versus-whole-model test, of the kind the stock gate runs, is well matched to the first category and poorly matched to this third one by construction, not by an implementation oversight: detecting it would require testing feature *subsets*, not individual features, against the whole model's power, a combinatorially larger and more expensive check than the one the gate currently runs. This is not a reason to expect the miss to be rare in practice. Real supply chain leakage of exactly this combination form is plausible and arguably common — an "actual fulfilment time" feature of the kind injected here is a realistic candidate for an accidentally retained column in a feature store that already contains the promised delivery window, and the two together reconstruct the label the same way they did in this battery's construction. A firm relying on the stock gate's leakage check as its sole defence against this class of error should not expect it to be caught.

## 6.6 The checklist is not a strawman; it is what many teams already do, and it is not nothing

It would be easy to read the metrics-and-checklist comparator as a deliberately weak baseline built to make the gate look good by contrast. It is not: a calibration-slope-and-performance-threshold review of the kind this paper's checklist formalises is, in the authors' own experience and consistent with the broader literature on how performance claims are typically reported (Van Calster et al., 2019), close to what many teams already do when a dedicated governance tool is not in use, and it is not nothing — it caught the Olist leaked-feature case through a calibration-slope deviation the leak happened to induce, a genuine, if incidental, success an AUROC-only rule could never have matched (Section 2.4). The checklist's failure mode in this battery is not that it is careless; it is that it is purely reactive to whatever a defect happens to do to a handful of aggregate numbers, with no mechanism aimed at the defect's actual source, which is why its one success (Olist) and one failure (the structurally identical defect on DataCo) on the same defect type is not a contradiction but the expected behaviour of a review that only ever sees symptoms. The stock gate's advantage over the checklist, where it has one, is specifically that several of its checks — schema contract, feature-level leakage ratio, split overlap — are aimed at a validation-methodology cause rather than a performance-metric symptom; Section 5.2–5.5 show this advantage is real for two of the four defects tested and absent for the other two, which is a more defensible and more useful claim than either "the gate is better" or "the gate adds nothing" stated without qualification.

## 6.7 What the critique that prompted this redesign got right, and what this battery adds beyond it

The methodological critique that motivated this redesign argued, among other points, that this paper's earlier evaluation conflated a configured rule firing with a demonstrated diagnosis, lacked a clear comparator, and had not tested whether the gate's coverage was complete once checks are silently skipped. Sections 5.2, 5.5 and 5.6 address each directly and with numbers rather than argument: BLOCKED-rate detection separated from NEEDS_REVIEW routing (addressing the first), a four-way comparison against AUROC-only and checklist baselines under identical cases (addressing the second), and an explicit skipped-check accounting showing 44–47% of checks contributed nothing to the verdict on every case in this battery (addressing the third). One point the critique raised that this battery does not resolve is a full human-reviewer comparison; consistent with the critique's own fallback guidance, this paper reports its comparator as a rule-based checklist standing in for a careful but non-specialist reviewer, and does not claim the gate, or any of the four approaches tested, is superior to an expert human reviewer working from the same evidence.

# 7. Limitations

The defects injected here are deliberately clean, single-mechanism constructions — a single leaked feature, a single fixed duplication rate, a single overlap channel per dataset, a single dropped column — chosen for interpretability rather than realism; Andrews, Briand and Labiche (2005) found that artificially seeded faults can behave differently from the real faults they approximate, and this paper has not tested whether a messier, multi-mechanism real-world defect would be caught, missed, or caught for a different reason than the clean cases tested here suggest. The battery uses three model configurations frozen from a prior study and does not re-tune any of them against this paper's own cases, which is a deliberate design choice (Section 4.1) but means the reported detection rates are specific to these configurations and may not generalise to a model tuned differently. The sampling-sensitivity comparison in Section 5.3 tests one model family (LightGBM) on two defect types; the capping effect almost certainly applies to other feature-level checks and other model families by the same mechanism, but this was not exhaustively tested. The frozen thresholds in Section 4.4, while genuinely fixed before evaluation and justified from the clean cases' own metric distribution, are nonetheless a single choice among many defensible ones, and a different AUROC-only threshold or calibration-slope band would shift the comparator baselines' apparent performance, though not, we expect, the qualitative pattern of which defects each approach structurally can and cannot see. Both datasets concern late-delivery prediction specifically; whether this battery's findings — particularly the single-feature-versus-combination leakage distinction and the capping artefact — generalise to other supply chain prediction tasks or to other governance tools is a question this design cannot answer. Finally, this paper tests one version of one tool (BDP Model Gate v0.6.0) under one configuration; its findings are evidence about that tool's current default behaviour and this paper's specific extension, not a general claim about automated governance tooling as a category.

Several further limitations are specific to this battery's small scale and are worth stating rather than leaving implicit in the numbers reported. Each defect-by-model-family cell in Section 5.2's table rests on a single constructed case, not a distribution of independently sampled defect instances of varying severity; the Olist group-overlap defect's small true magnitude (1.2% of the test set, against DataCo's 67%) is a property of that dataset's own repeat-customer rate within the specific test window chosen, not a parameter this paper varied deliberately across a range, and a systematic sweep of defect magnitude — rather than the single fixed magnitude used for each defect type here — would characterise the detection-versus-magnitude relationship Section 5.3 only demonstrates at two points. The random-forest confound documented in Section 5.2 (a pre-existing, defect-independent performance failure on Olist that renders that model family's verdicts uninformative for this battery's purpose) was identified only because the companion study's own prior results made it recognisable; a defect battery run without that context could easily have reported an inflated, confounded detection rate for Olist without noticing the confound at all, which is itself a caution about how readily a governance-evaluation result can look more conclusive than its construction supports. The checklist and AUROC-only comparators, while intended to represent realistic non-specialist review practice, were authored by the same team that built the gate integration being compared against them, and a comparator designed by a different team with a different view of what a "standard" review looks like might set different thresholds or compute different metrics, which could change the comparative picture in Section 5.6 without changing anything about the stock gate's own measured behaviour. And the extended check's apparent success (Section 5.4) rests on a battery in which its target defects happen to manifest as literal group-identifier overlap; a related-order leakage mechanism that did not involve any shared identifier at all — covariate similarity between genuinely distinct orders from the same customer, for instance, rather than a shared order or customer id — would not be caught by this specific extension, and this paper has not tested whether or how such a mechanism could be checked for at comparable cost.

# 8. Conclusion

Asked narrowly and testably — does this specific governance tool provide additional, actionable evidence for reviewing a late-delivery model before release, beyond what an AUROC-only or metrics-and-checklist review already provides — the answer this battery supports is a qualified yes, bounded to specific, named checks rather than a general endorsement. The stock BDP Model Gate reliably catches a schema mismatch no performance-only review can see at all, and reliably catches a group-dependence overlap once that overlap is large enough relative to whatever sample it is evaluated on; it misses a feature-combination leak regardless of sample size, and it would have appeared to miss a substantial, real exact-duplicate overlap had this paper not separately tested the same check on uncapped data and discovered that the miss was this paper's own sampling choice, not the tool's design. A one-function, cheaply computed extension recovers the sampling-induced gap without new false alarms, demonstrating that targeted, tested additions to a governance tool are both possible and verifiable rather than merely asserted. The practical guidance this paper offers a firm considering such a tool is correspondingly specific: trust its schema and large-overlap checks, do not trust its feature-level leakage check to see a combination leak, never row-cap a check whose logic depends on comparing training against test rows without separately testing whether the cap preserves that check's stated sensitivity, and read its BLOCKED rate and its NEEDS_REVIEW rate as two different signals rather than one aggregate verdict — because, on the evidence reported here, they are.

More broadly, this paper's own history is itself a demonstration of the standard it argues for. An earlier version of this study ran the same governance tool once, on the same two datasets, and reported its verdicts as evidence that the tool caught what it was configured to catch; a subsequent, external methodological critique pointed out, correctly, that a verdict is not yet evidence of a diagnosis, that the study lacked an independent comparator, and that its own sampling and analytical choices had not been separately tested for whether they, rather than the tool, were responsible for what the study reported as the tool's behaviour. Every one of those criticisms turned out, on direct testing, to identify a real issue: a genuinely fabricated significance statistic in an unrelated part of the companion study was corrected before this evaluation began; a genuine sampling artefact in this evaluation's own gate integration was found, quantified, and resolved by the uncapped comparison in Section 5.3; and the four-way comparator this paper reports throughout exists specifically because the original version had none. A governance tool is not the only thing in a release pipeline that benefits from being tested against its own stated claims rather than taken on faith — the research evaluating it does too, and this paper is better for having been required to meet the same standard it asks the tool to meet.
