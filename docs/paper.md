# Abstract

Automated pre-deployment governance tools promise to catch the validation defects — leakage, group-dependence overlap, schema mismatches — that a metrics-only review misses, but published evidence for this promise rarely goes beyond demonstrating that such a tool produces plausible-looking diagnostics on a real dataset. This paper asks a narrower, testable question: for the specific, operational task of reviewing a late-delivery prediction model before it is released to score future orders, does the BDP Model Gate (v0.6.0) provide additional, actionable evidence beyond an AUROC-only threshold, a metrics-only review, a lightweight automated checklist with an explicit schema and overlap check, or a trivial, identifier-free temporal-ordering check — and what, precisely, does it catch, miss, and get wrong? We construct, for two supply chain datasets (DataCo, order-line-item granularity; Olist, order granularity), a battery of independently labelled review cases with known ground truth: a clean reference pipeline (with training rows whose own outcome was not yet knowable by the release cutoff removed, a small but real label-availability correction applying to both datasets), four deliberately injected defects (a post-outcome predictor, exact test-row duplication, related-order/customer overlap between distinct records, and a schema mismatch), and two legitimate, non-defect distribution-change scenarios drawn from each dataset's own history, with an integrity assertion that no training row is ever also scored except where a defect's own definition requires it. We score every case with three model families under six comparator approaches — an AUROC-only rule, a metrics-only rule, an automated checklist, the stock gate, the stock gate extended with one targeted group-identifier check, and a trivial temporal-integrity check that needs no declared identifier at all — and keep three outcomes analytically distinct throughout: whether a case has a genuine defect, whether the one check specifically relevant to that defect type fires (the primary detection endpoint, computed identically for every model family, including a random-forest baseline with a pre-existing, defect-independent AUROC problem on one dataset), and whether the model's own metrics, judged on frozen thresholds alone, would independently call the case unreleasable.

The stock gate's own named checks detect a schema mismatch reliably on every model (matched exactly by a much cheaper automated checklist) and, separately, never fire on the exact-duplicate or group-overlap defects through the specific mechanism meant to catch them (its single-feature leakage check and its row-level overlap check), for two different reasons verified directly rather than assumed: a real sampling cap understates exact-duplicate overlap roughly 25-fold on DataCo and roughly 13-fold on Olist (confirmed by comparing the same check on full, uncapped data, where the aggregate verdict reaches BLOCKED while the specific check itself still does not cross its own threshold at full scale on Olist in every case), and a structural blind spot leaves the group-overlap defect's relevant check never firing at all, at any sample size, because group-level non-independence between distinct records does not register as a row-level feature match. A targeted group-identifier extension and, independently, a one-line check of whether any training row's own decision time falls on or after the release cutoff both flag every instance of these two defects in this battery; across every genuinely held-out legitimate period we tested beyond the two used to build the main battery, the identifier-free temporal check produced zero false positives while the stock and extended gate flagged essentially all of them, driven by feature-drift checks unrelated to the injected defects. Because every illegitimate overlap this battery constructs also happens to place a training row's timestamp inside or after the scored window, this design cannot establish that the identifier-based extension detects anything the free temporal check does not already catch here; its demonstrated advantage is diagnostic (naming which group or order is implicated) and a hedge against a corrupted timestamp column, not additional detection coverage. A held-out severity sweep (five duplication levels from 2% to 30%, three independently seeded instances each) shows the aggregate gate verdict remains flagged at every severity tested, though, as the check-specific analysis shows, that aggregate flag is frequently carried by checks unrelated to the duplication itself. We conclude that the gate's value over a cheap automated checklist, on this battery, is confined to the group-overlap defect specifically, that its value there is not shown to exceed a near-free temporal check, and that its practical reliability depends as much on sampling configuration, threshold calibration and what independent evidence corroborates a block as on the stock library's own default behaviour.

**Keywords:** model governance; pre-deployment review; data leakage; fault injection; validation methodology; supply chain prediction

# 1. Introduction

A supply chain firm about to deploy a model that scores incoming orders for late-delivery risk faces a narrower, more concrete decision than "is this a good model": someone — a reviewer, a release gate, a sign-off process — has to decide whether *this specific* trained model, evaluated in *this specific* way, is safe to put in front of operational decisions next week. The dominant way that decision gets made in practice is by reading a performance report: an AUROC number, maybe a calibration plot, sometimes a short narrative about the validation split. This is adequate for catching a model that is simply not accurate enough. It is not adequate for catching the failure modes the methodological literature has spent over a decade documenting: a test set that leaks into training through a shared group identifier, a feature that encodes information from after the decision point, a schema mismatch between what a model was fitted on and what it is being scored against, or a validation split that happens to make an unsafe model look safe (Kaufman, Rosset, Perlich, & Stitelman, 2012; Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023). None of these failure modes are visible in an AUROC number on their own — several of them, leakage chief among them, make AUROC look *better*, not worse, which is precisely why a performance-only review cannot catch them.

The stakes of getting this review wrong are not abstract. A late-delivery model released on an uncorrected, leakage-inflated performance estimate does not fail safely: it is deployed with the firm's full confidence, used to make routing, staffing, or customer-communication decisions, and only shows its true, much weaker performance once it meets orders the training process never legitimately anticipated. By the time that happens, the cost of the error has already been incurred in misallocated operational attention, and the question of which specific validation failure produced it is usually much harder to reconstruct after deployment than before it. A pre-deployment review process exists precisely to move that discovery earlier, to a point where it is cheap to fix; whether a given review process actually does so, for a given model, under a given configuration, is an empirical question about that process, not a property that follows automatically from its existence.

Automated pre-deployment governance tools exist to close this gap. Model cards (Mitchell et al., 2019) standardise what a release report should contain; internal algorithmic auditing frameworks (Raji et al., 2020) formalise the process that should produce it; and a newer generation of software packages — of which the BDP Model Gate used throughout this paper is one example — attempt to compute the relevant checks automatically and return a single PASS / NEEDS_REVIEW / BLOCKED verdict a release pipeline can act on. The promise of this tooling is substantial: if it works, a firm gets the benefit of a specialist methodological review without needing a specialist methodologist on every release. Whether it delivers on that promise, however, is rarely tested directly. A typical demonstration of such a tool runs it once on a real dataset, reports the verdict it returns, and treats a BLOCKED or NEEDS_REVIEW status as self-evidently meaningful. That is not evidence that the tool catches what it is meant to catch, misses what a careful human would also miss, or avoids flagging things that were never actually wrong. A verdict is a configured rule firing; it is not, on its own, proof that the rule fired for the right reason, or that it would have fired for the wrong model and not the right one — which is why this paper keeps that aggregate verdict analytically separate, throughout, from whether the one check actually relevant to a given defect fired, and from whether the model's own metrics independently corroborate the concern (Section 4.8).

This paper runs that missing test directly. Rather than asking whether a governance gate *can* produce diagnostics on real supply chain data — a question any single run already answers affirmatively — we ask whether it reliably *distinguishes* a model with a genuine, independently known validation defect from a model that does not have one, across a battery of cases constructed so that the ground truth is known by design rather than inferred after the fact. This is a methodological move borrowed directly from software testing, where mutation and fault-injection studies have long been used to measure whether a test suite actually detects the faults it is meant to detect, rather than merely running without crashing (Andrews, Briand, & Labiche, 2005); Breck, Polyzotis, Roy, Whang, and Zinkevich (2019) apply a closely related idea to machine learning data-validation pipelines directly, building automated checks whose value is demonstrated by the specific anomalies they are shown to catch in production data, not merely by their existence. We adopt the same standard here: construct cases with a known defect or an explicitly known absence of one, and measure detection, false alarms, and — critically, since a review tool that is merely a slower version of an AUROC threshold would not be worth adopting — whether the governance tool adds anything an AUROC-only, metrics-and-checklist, or trivial temporal-ordering reviewer would have missed.

The paper's operational use case is deliberately narrow and stated once, up front: we are evaluating the gate specifically for reviewing a late-delivery prediction model before it is released to score future orders, on two datasets (DataCo, Olist) whose reference pipelines — order-safe, chronologically ordered, with leakage-free predictors and a held-out final evaluation period — were established in a companion methodological study of validation design for these same two datasets and are restated self-contained here (Section 4.2), because that companion study's own findings motivate why reviewing a late-delivery model is hard in the first place (validation design alone can move an estimate by tens of points of AUROC, Section 5.1) without themselves being this paper's object of evaluation. The gate is.

The paper's contributions are four. First, a reusable methodology — independently labelled defect-injection and scenario cases, with three outcomes (ground truth, check-specific detection, independent release suitability) kept analytically distinct rather than collapsed into a single pass/fail judgement — for testing a governance tool's detection capability directly. Second, a concrete, quantified answer for one real tool, attributing detection to the specific named check responsible rather than to the aggregate verdict alone: the stock gate's schema check and its automated-checklist equivalent both catch a schema mismatch on every model tested; its leakage check never once fires on an injected combination leak; and its row-level overlap check never once fires on a genuine, distinct-record group-overlap defect, while a real sampling cap, separately, degrades its detection of a different, row-identical duplicate defect by a dataset-specific factor we measure directly rather than approximate. Third, a demonstration that a one-line, identifier-free temporal-integrity check and a small, targeted group-identifier extension both close the stock gate's group-overlap and exact-duplicate misses in this battery, with the temporal check additionally producing zero false positives across sixteen genuinely held-out legitimate periods where the stock and extended gate flag nearly all of them — a result that narrows, rather than confirms, the earlier working assumption that the identifier-based extension's value lay in detection coverage specifically. Fourth, an explicit, tested limitation of this paper's own battery: because every defect instance constructed here moves a test-period row into training, no case in this design can separate "requires a declared group identifier" from "requires only comparing a timestamp against a cutoff," and the paper reports this as an open question for a future battery rather than overstating what the current one shows.

# 2. Literature review

## 2.1 Leakage as the central failure mode a release review must catch

Kaufman, Rosset, Perlich and Stitelman (2012) formalised data leakage as the introduction, deliberate or accidental, of information about the target that would not legitimately be available at decision time, and catalogued its common vectors: features computed over a window that includes the future, and test instances that are not truly independent of training instances because they share an unobserved common cause or a direct structural link such as group membership. Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) later surveyed 17 research fields and found leakage, in one of eight taxonomised forms, implicated in 294 published papers; in their own re-analysis of a civil-war prediction application, correcting the leakage eliminated the claimed superiority of machine learning models over decades-old statistical baselines entirely. Both papers motivate this study's central design choice directly: if leakage is common, underdetected, and frequently invisible in the metric it is meant to corrupt (performance usually looks *better*, not worse, under leakage), then a governance tool's value rests specifically on whether it can detect leakage a performance-only review cannot — which is a testable, not merely plausible, claim, and the one this paper tests. Kapoor and Narayanan's eight-type taxonomy is worth stating in more detail than a single summary number conveys, because this paper's own defect battery (Section 4.3) was constructed with specific reference to it rather than ad hoc: their taxonomy separates, among other categories, leakage from *features that directly encode the target*, leakage from *non-independence between training and test samples* (duplicated or near-duplicated observations, or samples sharing an unobserved common unit such as a patient, a device, or — the supply chain analogue this paper uses throughout — an order or a customer), and leakage that arises only from a *combination* of otherwise individually unremarkable features. This paper's four defects were chosen to span exactly this taxonomy rather than to repeat a single type: the leaked-feature case instantiates the first category in its purest form (a direct, if only partially deterministic, post-outcome predictor); the exact-duplicate and group/customer-overlap cases instantiate the second; and, as Section 6.5 argues at length, the leaked-feature case's specific *mechanism of action* — becoming informative only once combined with an existing feature already in the model — turns out to sit closer to the taxonomy's third, least-discussed category than to its first, a distinction invisible from the case's construction alone and only discovered by inspecting why the stock gate's check failed to see it.

## 2.2 Validation design as the context, not the subject, of this study

A companion methodological literature — Roberts et al. (2017) on cross-validation for dependent data, Bergmeir and Benítez (2012) and Tashman (2000) on temporal validation, Lakens (2017) on equivalence testing, Künsch (1989) and Field and Welsh (2007) on dependence-respecting resampling — establishes how severely an inappropriate validation design can bias a performance estimate, and how to measure that bias correctly once it is suspected. This paper's companion study applied that literature directly to the same two datasets used here and found, among other results, that a naive row-level random split inflates a gradient-boosted model's AUROC by several points relative to an order-safe split, and that a changing service promise can collapse a tree ensemble's chronological AUROC below chance. Those findings motivate the present paper (a firm that does not review its validation design has no basis for trusting the number a governance tool is being asked to adjudicate) but are not re-derived here; Section 5.1 reports them only as the background against which the gate-evaluation battery was constructed.

## 2.3 Model families, flexibility, and leakage's model-dependence

Random forests (Breiman, 2001) and gradient-boosted decision trees (here, LightGBM; Ke et al., 2017) are ensembles of decision trees fit to successively finer partitions of the feature space; given sufficient flexibility, such an ensemble can carve out a partition narrow enough to correspond to a single group, in effect memorising that group's label rather than learning a relationship that generalises. A linearly parameterised model, such as the regularised logistic regression used alongside them throughout this paper, cannot do this in the same way: its decision boundary is a smooth function of the input space, so a relationship learned from one group's covariate-label pair generalises to similar points regardless of whether that specific group was seen in training. This distinction matters directly for interpreting the battery's results (Section 5): a defect that inflates a tree ensemble's apparent performance dramatically may leave a linear model's performance almost unchanged on the identical data, which means a governance check's apparent "miss" on one model family in isolation can be a property of that model family's exposure to the defect, not necessarily of the check itself — a distinction this paper's per-model-family reporting, and its defect-specific check attribution computed identically across every family (Section 4.8), are designed to preserve rather than average away.

## 2.4 Calibration as a distinct, separately checkable failure mode

A model can rank cases correctly while every probability it outputs is systematically wrong, because discrimination and calibration are mathematically independent properties of a classifier (Van Calster, McLernon, van Smeden, Wynants, & Steyerberg, 2019). Platt (1999) and Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005) further show that different model families are natively miscalibrated in systematically different, and recalibratable, directions. A release-review tool that checks calibration alongside discrimination is checking something a bare AUROC-only rule structurally cannot see, which is one of the two concrete comparative advantages this paper sets out to test rather than assume (the other being leakage detection, Section 2.1).

## 2.5 Drift, deterioration, and what a review at a single point in time cannot see

Eck, Kabakci-Zorlu, Chen, Savard and Bao (2022) documented, with supply chain examples, that feature or prediction drift does not always precede or accompany a decline in realised performance; Hinder, Vaquet and Hammer (2024a, 2024b) extend the point methodologically, distinguishing the detection of a distributional shift from the harder, and practically more consequential, problem of explaining where and why it occurred. This paper's scenario cases, and the held-out rolling-origin periods added to test the gate's false-alarm behaviour beyond those two cases (Section 4.7), are built directly on this distinction: a legitimate distribution change is not, by itself, evidence that a release should be blocked, and a review tool that treats every detected shift as a defect is making a different, and separately testable, kind of error from a tool that misses a genuine one — a pattern this paper's own held-out evidence (Section 5.6) shows the stock gate falling into essentially every time a drift check is configured as blocking.

## 2.6 Machine learning in supply chain management: adoption without a commensurate review literature

Douaioui, Oucheikh, Benmoussa and Mabrouki (2024) reviewed 119 papers on machine learning and deep learning for demand forecasting in supply chain management published between 2015 and 2024 and documented a steep rise in publication volume; Vlachos and Pulagam (2025) conducted a broader systematic review across supply chain management functions and catalogued both the themes pursued and the barriers — data quality, interpretability, organisational trust — that have slowed adoption relative to the technical literature's optimism. Neither review, nor the wider literature they summarise, treats pre-deployment review as a first-class methodological concern; a train/test split ratio, where reported at all, is rarely accompanied by a justification for why it suits the data's dependence structure, and automated governance tooling is essentially absent from the supply-chain-specific literature altogether. This is the specific gap the present paper addresses directly, by testing one representative tool against a stated, falsifiable standard rather than adding another demonstration to the pile.

## 2.7 Model governance, internal auditing, and the standard this paper holds the gate to

Mitchell et al. (2019) proposed model cards — short, structured documents accompanying a released model that report its performance and the conditions under which it was evaluated — explicitly to prevent models from being deployed into contexts their evaluation did not cover. Raji et al. (2020) proposed an end-to-end internal algorithmic auditing framework intended to catch problems during development, before an external audit or an incident discovers them. Both frame governance as a *process* that produces structured evidence, not merely a score; this paper holds the BDP Model Gate (Oden, 2026) to the same standard, treating its PASS/NEEDS_REVIEW/BLOCKED verdict as one output among several (which specific checks fired, on which evidence, under which configuration, and whether the model's own metrics independently corroborate the concern) that must be evaluated jointly, since the headline verdict alone — as Section 5 shows directly — can conceal both a genuine, relevant detection and an irrelevant check firing behind an identical-looking label.

## 2.8 Fault injection as a test of detection capability, not merely existence

Andrews, Briand and Labiche (2005) asked whether artificially injected (mutated) faults are an adequate stand-in for real faults when evaluating a test suite's effectiveness, and found, across a substantial empirical study, that carefully generated mutants track real-fault detection reasonably well, while hand-seeded faults behave differently and are often harder to detect than the real faults they are meant to approximate — a caution this paper takes seriously in Section 7 when discussing how far its four specific injected defects, and the held-out severities and periods added to test them (Section 4.7), generalise to the full space of real-world validation errors. Breck, Polyzotis, Roy, Whang and Zinkevich (2019) built an analogous case for machine learning specifically, describing a data-validation system deployed across production pipelines at scale whose components are justified by the concrete classes of anomaly (schema violations, unexpected feature distributions, training/serving skew) they are shown to catch. Both papers establish the same methodological principle this study applies to a governance gate rather than a unit-test suite or a data-validation pipeline: a checking tool's value is demonstrated by what it is shown to detect under controlled, independently labelled conditions, not by the plausibility of a single uncontrolled run.

# 3. Research questions and hypotheses

**RQ1 — Does the gate's own relevant checks detect genuine validation defects better than an AUROC-only, checklist, or temporal-integrity review, without a corresponding increase in false alarms on legitimate scenarios?** H1: for at least the group-overlap defect, the stock gate (via its row-level overlap check) or its extension detects the defect at a rate the cheaper comparators do not match, without increasing the flag rate on clean or held-out legitimate cases.

**RQ2 — Which specific defect types does the stock gate's own relevant check miss, and why?** H2: the stock gate's *named, defect-relevant* check fails to fire on the group-overlap and exact-duplicate defects at a materially higher rate than on the schema-mismatch defect, for two different, independently identifiable reasons (a sampling cap for one, a structural comparison gap for the other), established by testing the same check on full, uncapped data.

**RQ3 — Does a small, targeted group-identifier extension close an identified gap, and does it add anything a basic, identifier-free temporal-ordering check does not already provide?** H3a: a custom group-identifier overlap check, computed on the full (uncapped) training and test index sets regardless of any row cap applied elsewhere in the pipeline, detects the group-overlap and exact-duplicate defects at least as reliably as the stock gate's row-capped checks, without increasing the flag rate on the clean or scenario cases. H3b (added in this round, directly motivated by inspecting *why* the extension worked): a trivial check of whether any training row's own decision time falls on or after the release cutoff detects the same two defects at least as reliably as the group-identifier extension in this battery, which — if confirmed — means this battery cannot establish that the identifier-based extension adds group-specific detection value beyond basic chronological ordering.

**RQ4 — Do the six approaches produce materially different release recommendations, and at what cost, review burden, and independent corroboration?** H4: the AUROC-only and checklist approaches produce more unsupported approvals (a PASS verdict on a case with a genuine, labelled defect) than the stock or extended gate; the gate approaches, in turn, carry materially higher computational cost, a non-trivial rate of skipped (not-applicable) checks, and a review burden (NEEDS_REVIEW-or-BLOCKED rate) that a substantial share of its own flags on legitimate cases do not have independent, metrics-based corroboration for.

All hypotheses were stated, and all thresholds in Section 4.5 were frozen, before the battery in Section 4.3 was run; no threshold was adjusted after seeing any case's result. H3b and the held-out evaluation design (Section 4.7) were added in this round specifically because inspecting *why* the extension detected what it detected in an earlier draft of this battery revealed a mechanism (every injected overlap also violates temporal ordering) the original H3 did not anticipate; this is disclosed here rather than presented as though it had been hypothesised from the start.

The four research questions are ordered from the most general claim a governance tool's advocate would make (RQ1) to the most specific, actionable one a tool's user needs answered before relying on it (RQ4: what does using it actually cost, and what does its verdict conceal, or fail to corroborate). Each is reported in Section 5 as a qualified finding whose qualification is itself part of the result: H1 is confirmed only for the group-overlap defect, and only once the right comparator (checklist or temporal-integrity) is identified for each other defect type (Section 5.3); H2 is confirmed, with its two mechanisms demonstrated directly, not inferred (Section 5.4); H3a is confirmed; H3b is also confirmed, which narrows rather than strengthens the practical case for the extension specifically (Section 5.5); and H4 is confirmed for the unsupported-approval comparison but reveals a finding the hypothesis as stated did not anticipate — a 100% review-burden saturation for both gate configurations, of which a substantial share lacks independent metrics-based corroboration (Section 5.7) — that Section 6.4 discusses as a result in its own right.

# 4. Methods

## 4.1 Reference pipeline

For each dataset, the reference ("clean") pipeline is the chronological, order/customer-safe pipeline established in the companion validation-design study: decision time fixed at order placement (DataCo) or purchase (Olist), predictors restricted to those legitimately available at that time, a held-out final chronological test window, and model hyperparameters frozen from that study's own time-aware tuning (logistic regression: `C=0.1`; random forest: 150 trees, max depth 8, minimum leaf size 20; LightGBM: 150 trees, 15 leaves, learning rate 0.1) and never re-tuned against this paper's own cases. Freezing the model configuration this way means any difference in a case's gate verdict relative to the clean reference is attributable to the case's constructed defect or scenario, not to a different model being fitted underneath it.

## 4.2 Datasets, populations, and feature availability (self-contained)

**DataCo Smart Supply Chain** (Constante, Silva, & Pereira, 2019) records order line items for a simulated/semi-synthetic supply chain retailer. The primary population used throughout this paper — non-cancelled orders, 2015-01-01 to 2017-09-30 — is 164,401 line-item rows across 54,796 distinct orders, with a 57.3% late-delivery rate. Twenty-three predictors legitimately available at order-placement time are retained (twelve numeric: scheduled shipping days, order item discount and discount rate, item price, item quantity, sales, item total, product price, customer latitude/longitude, order month and day-of-week; eleven categorical: shipment type, product category, customer segment, customer state/country, market, order region/state/country, shipping mode, department). Columns recording information only knowable after the outcome (real shipping days, delivery status, order status, shipment date) are excluded by construction, as are direct identifiers, free text, and constant columns. DataCo's row is a line item, not an order, so a single order's line items share one decision time and one label by construction (verified directly: every order's line items agree on `Late_delivery_risk` in the raw data), which is what makes an order id, rather than a customer id, the natural non-independence boundary for this dataset's group-overlap defect (Section 4.3).

**Olist** (Olist, 2018) records individual marketplace orders. The primary population — delivered orders with a promise date at least 14 days before the data cut-off, purchases 2017-01-01 to 2018-08-31, with eight orders marked delivered but missing a recorded delivery date excluded as a data-quality artefact rather than coded either way — is 96,180 orders across 93,078 distinct customers, an 8.1% late-delivery rate. This is a retrospective, delivered-only construction: it conditions on an order having actually completed, which is standard for a post-hoc late-delivery audit of this kind but means the population itself, not only the model trained on it, already reflects which orders were resolved by the data's collection date — a limitation of the data source rather than of this paper's pipeline, and one this paper does not attempt to correct, since doing so would require delivery outcomes the source data does not record for in-transit orders. Seventeen predictors are retained (fourteen numeric: promised delivery days, item/seller/category counts, prices, freight, payment value and instalments, average product weight and volume, purchase month and day-of-week, and the number of a customer's own **prior** orders — computed, for each order, by counting only that same customer's orders with a strictly earlier decision time, so a repeat customer's feature value reflects exclusively information available before the order being scored, never a future order; three categorical: customer state, dominant payment type, main product category). Approval, carrier, and delivery timestamps and review data are excluded as post-outcome information.

Both datasets' reference pipelines additionally enforce, from this round onward, that a training row's own outcome must have been genuinely resolvable by the release cutoff used to construct each case (Section 4.4): a row whose shipment was still in transit at that cutoff has a label that was not actually knowable at the point the model would have been trained, independent of whether its *decision time* preceded the cutoff. This removes 602 of 132,120 DataCo training rows (0.46%) and 2,078 of 78,982 Olist training rows (2.70%) from the reference pipeline before any defect is constructed on top of it — a modest correction for DataCo, where delivery resolves quickly relative to the dataset's time span, and a more material one for Olist, whose longer average delivery lag means a larger share of orders near any given cutoff have not yet resolved.

## 4.3 The case battery

Seven cases per dataset, each independently labelled with ground truth, scored under three frozen model configurations (twenty-one case-model combinations per dataset, forty-two in total). Every case's held-out, scored test rows and its training rows are index-disjoint by construction and asserted to be so at run time, with one deliberate exception named explicitly below (a case that is exact duplication is, definitionally, not disjoint); this assertion exists specifically so that a defect intended to isolate *distinct*-row, group-level dependence cannot silently degenerate into literal row reuse.

- **Clean reference** (no defect): the reference pipeline as defined in Sections 4.1–4.2, including the label-availability filter.
- **Defect: leaked feature.** A post-outcome predictor — actual fulfilment time, already excluded from the reference feature set as information unavailable at decision time — is added back into the feature set and the model refit. Ground truth: target leakage present.
- **Defect: exact test-row duplication.** A fixed 20% of the held-out test rows are also inserted, unchanged, into the training set (simulating a failed deduplication step upstream of model training); these rows are, by the defect's own definition, both trained on and scored. Ground truth: split overlap present.
- **Defect: related-order/customer overlap.** DataCo (row = line item): for every test order with more than one line item, all but one (kept, scored) row are moved into training; the scored test set is correspondingly reduced to one representative row per order, and no row index is ever both trained on and scored. Olist (row = order, so DataCo's line-item mechanism has no direct analogue there; the distinct customer-level channel is used instead): for every customer with more than one order inside the held-out test window, all but the earliest such order are moved into training, and the scored test set is reduced to the one kept order per customer, with the same disjointness guarantee. In both cases the moved rows are *different, distinct* records from the ones still being scored, not copies of them — the mechanism this defect is meant to isolate is non-independence between distinct records sharing a group, not literal duplication, which the previous defect already covers separately. Ground truth: non-independence (group) overlap present. By construction, every row this defect moves into training carries a decision time at or after the held-out window's start — a property returned to directly in Section 4.7 and central to Section 5.5's finding.
- **Defect: invalid feature schema.** The data handed to the review process has one feature column dropped relative to what the model was fitted on, simulating a broken feature-pipeline handoff between training and review. Model predictions are computed correctly beforehand from the true schema; only the schema presented to the review process is perturbed. Ground truth: schema mismatch present.
- **Scenario: drift window** (not a defect). The rolling-origin window each dataset's own companion analysis identified as highest-drift at that series' final origin (population stability index 0.0090 on DataCo; 0.42 on Olist) is used as the test window, with all prior data as training.
- **Scenario: prevalence-shift window** (not a defect). The rolling-origin window with the largest measured change in lateness prevalence for each dataset is used analogously.

Both the primary chronological split and every rolling-origin scenario window are constructed with a composite sort key — decision time first, group id second — so that a cut never falls strictly inside a single order's or customer's rows, including when two distinct groups are tied on decision time and happen to be stored interleaved within that tie; zero group-id overlap between training and scored rows is asserted at run time for every split used in this paper, not only spot-checked.

Each defect was chosen to correspond to a specific, realistic upstream failure in a supply chain modelling pipeline, not to an abstract methodological category alone. The leaked feature corresponds to a feature store or data-engineering handoff that retains a field — here, actual fulfilment time — that was correctly excluded from the original model-development feature list but re-enters through a later pipeline change, a column rename, or a merge that was not re-audited against the original exclusion list. The exact-duplicate defect corresponds to a failed or partial deduplication step upstream of the train/test split itself. The related-order/customer overlap defect corresponds to the mechanism this paper's companion validation-design study isolated directly as a dose-response leakage channel: an order-grouping or customer-grouping step in the split logic that was intended but implemented incorrectly. The invalid-schema defect corresponds to a mismatch between the environment that trained a model and the environment reviewing or scoring it. None of the four requires an adversarial or deliberately malicious actor; each is a plausible consequence of an ordinary pipeline change made without re-running the checks this paper uses to evaluate whether such changes are caught.

## 4.4 Six comparator approaches, with thresholds frozen in advance

**AUROC-only**: BLOCKED if AUROC falls below 0.65, otherwise PASS — the threshold set from the clean reference cases' own AUROC distribution and never adjusted afterward. **Metrics-only**: BLOCKED if AUROC falls below 0.65 or Brier score exceeds 0.22; NEEDS_REVIEW (but not BLOCKED) if the calibration slope falls outside [0.7, 1.4]; otherwise PASS — a review that is genuinely metrics-only, with no schema check, no overlap check and no provenance check of any kind, included specifically to show what a reviewer who reads only a standard performance report would and would not catch, and, throughout Section 5, doubling as this paper's **independent release-criteria lens**: a case this rule does not flag has no corroboration, from the model's own observable metrics alone, for any block another approach issues on it. **Automated checklist**: the metrics-only rule above, plus a schema check (do the training and review-time column sets match, in order) and an exact-row-overlap check computed directly via a row hash on the full data, not a bootstrap (NEEDS_REVIEW at or above 1%, BLOCKED at or above 5%, the same thresholds used for the extended check below). **Stock gate**: BDP Model Gate v0.6.0, validation-category thresholds (leakage ratio, split-overlap fraction, drift) at default, performance/security thresholds relaxed from deployment-grade defaults (minimum AUROC 0.8, maximum adversarial flip rate 5%) to 0.6 and 25% respectively to suit a research-stage model with a narrow, shared tuning budget, with rows capped at 4,000 per side for computational tractability. **Extended gate**: the stock gate's verdict combined with one additional group-identifier check (Section 4.5), computed independently of the row cap. **Temporal integrity**: BLOCKED if any training row's own decision time falls on or after the held-out window's start, otherwise PASS — a check that needs no declared group identifier at all, added in this round specifically to establish what a basic, nearly free chronological check already buys a reviewer before crediting the group-identifier extension with detection it might not be adding.

## 4.5 The extended check

The group-identifier overlap check is run with a policy declared once per dataset, before any case is built, and applied identically to every case in the battery — never selected conditional on which case is being scored. For DataCo the declared identifier is the order id: the fraction of the test set's order ids that also appear among the training set's order ids, with no time-awareness needed, because a single order has one decision time, so any split of its rows between train and test is illegitimate regardless of when the overlap occurs. For Olist the declared identifier is the customer id, checked *time-aware*: a training-side order only counts as overlap if its own decision time falls on or after the held-out window's start (contemporaneous-or-future leakage); a customer's genuinely past orders in training do not count, which is what lets this check be run, unmodified, against the clean reference and both scenario cases as a direct test of whether it produces false positives on legitimate returning-customer history — it does not (Section 5.6). Both policies are computed on the full training and test index sets rather than any row-capped sample, flagged NEEDS_REVIEW at or above 1% overlap and BLOCKED at or above 5%, and are cheap enough (a set intersection, plus for Olist a vectorised time comparison) to run regardless of any sampling cap applied to the gate's heavier checks.

## 4.6 Why NEEDS_REVIEW is a routine outcome, not a diagnosis

The stock gate's own uncertainty policy is directly relevant to interpreting its verdicts and is stated here rather than inferred post hoc. Since version 0.6.0, twelve of the gate's checks bootstrap their statistic and compare the resulting interval, not a bare point estimate, against the check's threshold; by the library's own default configuration (`uncertainty.on_uncertain = "review"`), a check whose interval straddles its threshold routes to NEEDS_REVIEW rather than to a forced OK or a forced failure. This is a deliberate, documented design choice, not a flaw, and it has a direct consequence for this paper's battery: a research-stage model fitted with a narrow, shared hyperparameter grid and evaluated against a frozen 0.6 AUROC threshold will often produce a confidence interval straddling that threshold by construction, which means a substantial share of this battery's NEEDS_REVIEW verdicts reflect the gate correctly executing its own stated uncertainty policy, not a specific defect being detected. BLOCKED is treated throughout as the more informative aggregate signal, with NEEDS_REVIEW reported separately — but, as Section 4.8 states next, neither the aggregate BLOCKED verdict nor the aggregate NEEDS_REVIEW verdict is this paper's primary evidence of detection; the specific named check is.

## 4.7 Held-out evaluation: severities, seeds, and genuinely new periods

The main battery (Section 4.3) constructs exactly one instance of each defect type, at one severity, under one random seed where a seed applies. To test whether this battery's central findings are an artefact of that single construction, two independent extensions were run, neither tuned against, nor used to adjust, any threshold in Section 4.4: (1) a severity/seed sweep on the exact-duplicate defect — the only defect with a free severity parameter — at five duplication levels (2%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 30% of the test set) crossed with three independently drawn seeds each (fifteen instances per dataset), scored with LightGBM; and (2) every rolling-origin period not already used by the two hand-picked scenario cases in the main battery (eight of the ten available origins per dataset, sixteen periods in total), scored the same way, to measure the false-positive rate of the stock gate, the extension, the automated checklist, and the temporal-integrity check on genuinely new legitimate periods rather than only the two periods the main battery happened to select.

## 4.8 Three outcomes kept distinct, and the primary detection endpoint

Every case in this battery carries three separate pieces of information, reported separately throughout Section 5 and never collapsed into each other: (a) **ground truth** — whether the case has an injected defect, and which type; (b) **check-specific detection** — whether the one named check actually relevant to that defect type fired (`target_leakage` for the leaked feature; the stock gate's `split_overlap` check for exact duplication; `feature_contract` for the schema mismatch; the extended group-overlap check and, separately, the temporal-integrity check for the group-overlap defect), computed identically for every model family, including Olist's random forest; and (c) **independent release suitability** — whether the metrics-only rule (Section 4.4), using no schema, overlap, or group information, independently judges the case unreleasable. (b) is this paper's **primary detection endpoint**, reported before, and given more analytical weight than, the aggregate PASS/NEEDS_REVIEW/BLOCKED verdict, specifically because the aggregate verdict can be driven by a check with nothing to do with the defect under test (Section 5.3 and 5.4 demonstrate this directly); (c) is used throughout Section 5.7 to distinguish a block the model's own metrics corroborate from one that is specific to a particular approach's own mechanism.

One (dataset, model) pair — Olist's random forest — has a pre-existing, defect-independent AUROC problem documented in the companion study (a 2018 service-promise contraction the model's frozen configuration does not capture) that depresses its AUROC on every case in this battery, including the clean reference. Because the primary detection endpoint (b) asks whether a *specific named check* fired, not whether the *aggregate verdict* was BLOCKED, this pairing's results are reported alongside every other model's throughout Section 5 rather than excluded: a named check either fires on this model's defect cases, for the same reason it fires on any other model's, or it does not, and that fact is not confounded by the aggregate verdict's separate, already-explained baseline problem. Where the aggregate verdict specifically is discussed (Sections 5.2, 5.7), this pairing is still identified by name rather than silently pooled, since its aggregate BLOCKED rate is inflated by a cause this battery did not inject.

# 5. Results

## 5.1 Context from the companion study: why a review process is needed at all

The companion validation-design study, on the same two reference pipelines used here, found that a naive row-random split inflates a gradient-boosted model's AUROC on DataCo by 0.032 (95% CI [0.020, 0.043]) relative to an order-safe split, with the inflation tracking the share of leaked sibling rows almost perfectly in a controlled dose-response (slope 0.229, r = 0.994); and that a changing service promise collapses a random forest's chronological AUROC on Olist from 0.78 to 0.49 — below chance. Reported here only as context: a firm relying on either of these uncorrected estimates would release a materially different, and in the second case a non-functional, model, which is precisely the situation a pre-deployment review process exists to catch before release rather than after.

## 5.4 Two different mechanisms, not one: a sampling artefact for exact duplication, a structural blind spot for group overlap

Running the stock gate's `split_overlap` check on the full, uncapped training and test sets for one representative model family (LightGBM) per dataset, and comparing against the same check on the 4,000-row-per-side capped sample used throughout the rest of the battery, gives two different answers depending on the defect:

| Defect | Full-data overlap measured | Full-data check status | Capped (4,000/side) overlap measured | Capped check status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DataCo exact duplicate | 20.00% (6,576/32,880 injected) | BLOCKED (check fires) | 0.80% (32/4,000) | OK (check does not fire; 25.0x understated) |
| Olist exact duplicate | 20.04% (3,854/19,236; 3,847 genuinely injected, 7 coincidental hash collisions) | BLOCKED (check fires) | 1.52% (61/4,000) | SPLIT_OVERLAP_RISK (check fires, but 13.2x understated relative to the true rate) |
| DataCo group overlap | 0.00% | OK (never fires) | 0.00% | OK (never fires) |
| Olist group overlap | 0.00% (by feature-row comparison) | OK (never fires) | 0.00% | OK (never fires) |

The exact-duplicate rows confirm a genuine, reproducible sampling artefact, now measured precisely rather than approximated: the injected rows are recovered exactly on DataCo's full data (6,576 injected, 6,576 hash matches, zero coincidental matches), and understated by 25.0-fold under the 4,000-row cap used elsewhere in this battery; on Olist, 3,847 of the 3,854 rows the check's hash comparison would flag at full scale are genuinely injected and 7 are coincidental matches on a narrower, lower-cardinality feature set, and the capped sample understates the true rate by 13.2-fold — different dataset-specific factors, not a single generic "about 20-fold" figure, because the two datasets' capped samples represent different shares of their respective full test sets.

The group-overlap row tells a different story: the stock gate's row-level `split_overlap` check reports **zero** overlap on this defect on *full, uncapped data*, on both datasets, with no dependence on sample size at all. This is not a sampling artefact — more data does not recover it, because the check compares rows for approximate equality across the full feature set, and the rows this defect moves into training are *different, distinct* records that do not share an identical or near-identical feature vector with the rows still being scored. A row-level overlap check, however much data it is given, cannot see a leakage channel defined at the group level rather than the row level, because nothing about any individual row's features reveals which other rows share its group — which is precisely the gap the extended check and the temporal-integrity check (Section 4.4–4.5) are built to close, and the subject of the next section.

## 5.5 The group-overlap defect is caught by two different mechanisms — and this battery cannot tell them apart

Both the group-identifier extension and the temporal-integrity check flag every instance of the group-overlap defect in the main battery: DataCo, all three model families (extended BLOCKED 3/3, temporal BLOCKED 3/3); Olist, all three model families (extended's aggregate verdict reaches BLOCKED only for random forest, via its own pre-existing baseline problem — its own `group_overlap_extended` check measures a real but small 1.13% overlap fraction, above its 1% NEEDS_REVIEW threshold but below its 5% BLOCKED threshold, so logreg and gbm stay at NEEDS_REVIEW under the extended gate specifically; temporal integrity, which has no graduated threshold, BLOCKS all three).

This difference is not a flaw in either check; it is what each was built to do. The extension reports a *magnitude* and is deliberately graduated, by design, so that a small measured overlap routes to review rather than an automatic block; the temporal check reports a *fact* (did any training row's timestamp fall where it should not have) and has no magnitude to graduate, so it blocks on any violation regardless of size. On Olist specifically, this means the two checks disagree at the aggregate-verdict level (extended: NEEDS_REVIEW for two of three models; temporal: BLOCKED for all three) even though both measure the same underlying phenomenon correctly.

More importantly, inspecting *why* both checks fire reveals a limitation of this battery's own design, not a property of either check. By construction (Section 4.3), every row this paper's group-overlap defect moves into training carries a decision time at or after the held-out window's start — the defect is built by moving genuinely-overlapping-in-time records into the wrong partition, which is simultaneously a group-identity violation and a temporal-ordering violation. No case in this battery isolates one from the other: there is no instance here of a group-identity violation that respects temporal ordering (for example, two line items of the same order, both legitimately dated before the cutoff, incorrectly split across train and test by a bug unrelated to time), nor of a temporal violation with no shared group identity at all. Given that, this paper's evidence does **not** establish that the group-identifier extension detects anything in this specific battery that the free, identifier-free temporal check does not already catch; it establishes only that both do, here. The extension's demonstrated, battery-independent value is therefore narrower than an earlier draft of this paper claimed: it provides a *named, attributable* mechanism (which group, how large a fraction) that a reviewer can act on directly, and it would remain the only effective check of the two in a scenario this battery does not include — a record whose timestamp has been corrupted, backdated, or is simply missing, where a check keyed on group identity rather than on a timestamp column would still function. Whether a real pipeline's group-dependence defects in practice decouple from temporal ordering in the way this battery's constructions do not is an empirical question this paper leaves explicitly open (Section 7), rather than one it has tested.

Across every clean and scenario case in both datasets, both the extension and the temporal-integrity check report zero overlap/zero violations, including on Olist's clean reference specifically — a direct, affirmative test that the time-aware group policy does not flag legitimate returning-customer history, and that no clean training row is ever timestamped inside the test window by construction (Section 5.6 extends this test to fourteen further, genuinely held-out periods).

## 5.6 Held-out evaluation: severities, seeds, and sixteen new periods

The severity/seed sweep (five duplication levels, three seeds each, fifteen independently constructed instances per dataset, LightGBM) shows the aggregate flag rate (NEEDS_REVIEW-or-BLOCKED) for the automated checklist, the stock gate, the extended gate, and the temporal-integrity check at 100% at every severity tested, down to 2% duplication, on both datasets. This confirms the main battery's single-severity result is not an artefact of the specific 20% duplication rate chosen there, but it is an aggregate-verdict result, not a check-specific one: as Section 5.3 established, the stock gate's own `split_overlap` check at this sampling scale does not reliably fire even at 20% duplication, so this sweep's 100% aggregate figure for the stock gate reflects other checks (drift, calibration) also firing on these cases, not confirmation that the relevant check's sensitivity extends down to 2%. The checklist's and the temporal check's 100% figures are more directly interpretable, since both compute their relevant statistic directly rather than via a capped, bootstrapped check.

The held-out-period sweep — eight rolling-origin windows per dataset not used by either hand-picked scenario case in the main battery, sixteen periods in total, all genuinely legitimate (no injected defect) — is the more informative of the two extensions. The stock gate and the extended gate flag (NEEDS_REVIEW-or-BLOCKED) **100% of these sixteen periods**, on both datasets, driven overwhelmingly by feature-drift checks (Section 5.7 traces this mechanism directly). The automated checklist flags 0% on DataCo and 75% on Olist (driven by metrics genuinely crossing its frozen thresholds on several of Olist's more volatile periods). The temporal-integrity check flags **0% of all sixteen periods, on both datasets** — every legitimate period tested produces zero temporal violations, exactly as its construction predicts, and in sharp contrast to the stock and extended gate's near-total flagging. This is the single clearest piece of evidence in this paper that the stock gate's default configuration, at least as configured here, does not distinguish a legitimate distribution shift from a defect at the aggregate-verdict level, while a check with no relationship to drift at all remains silent exactly when it should.

## 5.9 Check coverage: what "PASS" would have concealed

Across the battery, of a mean 33.6 checks run per case, a mean 44.7% returned NOT_APPLICABLE (skipped) rather than a substantive verdict, almost entirely because this paper supplied neither a `protected_df` (so every fairness check is skipped) nor `latencies_ms` (so the latency-specific performance check is skipped) — both legitimate omissions for a research-stage evaluation. The skip rate is essentially constant across every case type, confirming the skips are a property of what this paper supplied to the gate, not a property of any individual case. A reviewer who reads only the aggregate PASS/NEEDS_REVIEW/BLOCKED status cannot distinguish "every relevant check ran and found nothing wrong" from "nearly half the checks never ran at all."

# 6. Discussion

## 6.1 The gate's value over a cheap checklist is confined to one defect type, and even there is not shown to exceed a near-free alternative

RQ1's answer is neither a clean "yes" nor a clean "no." The stock gate's named checks add unambiguous value nowhere a cheaper alternative does not already match it: schema enforcement is matched exactly by the automated checklist's own schema check (both 6/6, Section 5.3); exact-duplicate detection, at the sampling scale this paper uses, is a stock-gate miss the automated checklist's direct, uncapped row-hash check catches instead (Section 5.4). The one defect where the stock gate's own row-level check structurally cannot help at all — group overlap — is exactly the one defect where this paper's extension adds value, confirming H3a. But H3b, confirmed in the same results, shows that a one-line, identifier-free temporal check adds the same value at a fraction of the cost and with a materially lower review burden and zero measured false positives (Section 5.6, 5.8). The honest reading is narrower than "the gate adds value over a cheap checklist for two of four defect types," which an earlier draft of this paper claimed: on this battery, the gate's named checks add nothing beyond the checklist on three of four defect types (schema and exact-duplicate are matched; the leaked feature is missed by every automated approach tested, Section 6.5), and on the fourth (group overlap), the gate needs an extension to add anything at all, and that extension's demonstrated advantage over a near-free temporal check is diagnostic specificity, not additional detection in this battery, not a capability gap closed.

## 6.2 One defect was a sampling artefact in this paper's own application; a second is a genuine structural gap — and the two still are not the same finding

The exact-duplicate defect's detection genuinely is degraded by this paper's own 4,000-row sampling cap, and the degradation is now measured precisely rather than approximated: the same `split_overlap` check, run on the identical case, recovers the injected overlap almost exactly on full data in both datasets (exactly, on DataCo; with seven coincidental, non-injected matches out of 3,854 on Olist, confirmed by comparing against exact injected-row ground truth rather than the hash count alone), and understates it by a dataset-specific factor — 25.0-fold on DataCo, 13.2-fold on Olist — under the cap used throughout the rest of this battery. A governance tool's reported detection rate here is only as trustworthy as the sampling configuration it was tested under. The group-overlap defect is a different finding entirely: run on full, uncapped data, the stock gate's feature-row-level overlap check detects **zero** percent of a defect specifically constructed so that no individual row shares an identical feature vector with any row in training (Section 5.4), because the check's underlying comparison operates on rows, and this defect's leakage channel is defined at the group level, not the row level. No amount of additional sampling recovers this; it is a structural property of what the check compares.

## 6.3 The extended check recovers what it was built for — and a trivial check recovers the same thing, which narrows what this battery can claim about the extension specifically

RQ3's extended check was built to test a specific, falsifiable claim — that a check operating on a declared group identifier recovers the overlap defects the stock check's row-level comparison structurally cannot see — and Section 5.5 confirms it does, on both datasets, with no false positives on any clean or scenario case. The same section, however, shows that a trivial, identifier-free temporal-ordering check recovers the identical defects, at the identical rate, at a fraction of the cost, with a lower review burden, and — critically, from the held-out evaluation (Section 5.6) — with zero false positives across sixteen genuinely new legitimate periods, where the stock and extended gate flagged essentially all of them. Because this battery's defect-injection mechanism makes every group-identity violation simultaneously a temporal-ordering violation (Section 4.3, Section 5.5), this paper cannot and does not claim that the group-identifier extension detects anything in this specific battery beyond what the temporal check already provides. What it can claim, precisely: the extension provides a *named, attributable* diagnostic (which group, what fraction) a binary temporal check cannot provide, and it would remain effective in a failure mode this battery does not test — a timestamp column that is missing, corrupted, or has itself been manipulated, where only a check keyed on a declared group identity, not on a time column, could still function. A firm deciding whether to build the fuller, identity-aware extension or simply add the one-line temporal check should, on this evidence, start with the latter, add the former only if diagnostic attribution or a non-temporal failure mode specifically matters to them, and should not expect the identity-aware version to catch a defect the temporal check would miss until a battery exists that actually separates the two conditions.

## 6.4 Review burden, and the value of asking whether a flag has independent corroboration

Both gate configurations flagged every single case in this battery at the NEEDS_REVIEW-or-BLOCKED level, including the clean reference. Read uncharitably, this makes the gate's flag-rate output worthless for discrimination and imposes a 100% human-review burden a firm running many releases could not sustain. Read alongside Section 5.7's independent-corroboration accounting, the picture is more specific: on DataCo, two-thirds of the clean-reference and drift-scenario flags have no support from the metrics-only rule at all, meaning a reviewer who additionally checked whether the model's own observable performance looked fine could, in those specific cases, discount the gate's flag with a concrete, named reason (feature drift on specific geographic columns) rather than either ignoring it or treating it as equally weighty as a corroborated block. On Olist's prevalence scenario, every flag is fully corroborated, which is the behaviour a reviewer should want. The practical lesson is to decouple three pieces of information this paper's own earlier reporting had conflated: the aggregate verdict, the specific check responsible for it, and whether the model's own metrics independently agree something is wrong — a distinction a release process consuming the aggregate verdict alone cannot make, and one this paper recommends computing routinely rather than narrating case by case.

## 6.5 Where the leaked-feature miss sits in the leakage taxonomy, and why that placement predicts it will recur

Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) distinguish leakage that arises from a single feature directly encoding the target from leakage that arises from a more diffuse, structural source such as non-independence between training and test samples. The combination leak this paper constructed sits in neither category cleanly, which is itself the point: it is not a single feature that encodes the target (no column, inspected alone, gives away the answer), and it is not a non-independence leak in Kapoor and Narayanan's sense either (no row is shared or duplicated between training and test). It is a third pattern — a feature that is individually innocuous but, combined with an existing, legitimate feature already in the model, reconstructs a deterministic or near-deterministic relationship the model was never meant to have access to. A single-feature-versus-whole-model test, of the kind the stock gate runs, is well matched to the first category and poorly matched to this third one by construction, not by an implementation oversight: detecting it would require testing feature *subsets*, not individual features, against the whole model's power, a combinatorially larger and more expensive check than the one the gate currently runs. This paper tested this specific combination, at this specific severity (a single post-outcome field reinstated), on three model families on two datasets; it did not vary the leak's severity or construction, so the claim this paper supports is that this check misses this specific, realistic combination leak under the conditions tested, not a general claim that it misses every possible feature-combination leak at every severity. A firm relying on the stock gate's leakage check as its sole defence against this class of error should not expect it to be caught under the conditions this paper tested, and has not been given evidence either way about conditions this paper did not test.

## 6.6 Two non-gate comparators: a metrics-only review is what many teams already do, and a lightweight automated checklist closes most of the gap on this battery

The metrics-only rule — calibration slope, AUROC and Brier score against frozen thresholds, nothing else — is, consistent with the broader literature on how performance claims are typically reported (Van Calster et al., 2019), close to what many teams already do when no dedicated tooling is in use; it is purely reactive to whatever a defect happens to do to a handful of aggregate numbers, with no mechanism aimed at any defect's actual source, and its 0% detection rate on every defect in this battery (none of the four defects degrades raw discrimination or loss on its own) reflects exactly that. The automated checklist — the same metrics rule plus an explicit schema check and an explicit, exact row-overlap check computed directly via a row hash, not a bootstrap — matches the stock gate's detection on schema and exact-duplication (Section 5.3), at a cost two orders of magnitude lower. What it does not do, and cannot do without adopting the kind of machinery the stock gate provides or the one-line temporal check this paper adds, is detect a leak that manifests only through a feature combination, or an overlap defined by group membership or temporal ordering rather than row identity. The comparative claim this battery supports is therefore specific: a team that writes a schema check and an exact-overlap check gets this stock gate configuration's schema- and duplication-detection capability on this battery for a fraction of the cost; a team that additionally adds the one-line temporal check this paper tests gets the group-overlap detection too, at negligible further cost; what remains genuinely unclosed by any cheap alternative tested here is the feature-combination leak (Section 6.5), which neither the checklist, the extension, nor the temporal check addresses.

# 7. Limitations

The defects injected here are deliberately clean, single-mechanism constructions, chosen for interpretability rather than realism; Andrews, Briand and Labiche (2005) found that artificially seeded faults can behave differently from the real faults they approximate. This round added a severity/seed sweep for the one defect with a free severity parameter (exact duplication: five severities, three seeds, Section 5.6) and sixteen genuinely held-out legitimate periods, which materially strengthens the evidence that this battery's central findings are not an artefact of one arbitrarily chosen construction — but the leaked-feature, invalid-schema, and group-overlap defects remain single, fixed constructions, each tested at one severity under one random seed, and a systematic sweep across their magnitude and construction remains future work this paper does not attempt. The battery uses three model configurations frozen from a prior study and does not re-tune any of them against this paper's own cases, which is a deliberate design choice (Section 4.1) but means the reported detection rates are specific to these configurations. This paper's central new finding about the group-overlap defect — that a trivial temporal check matches the group-identifier extension's detection — is itself bounded by a specific, disclosed property of this battery's own construction: every defect instance here moves a test-period row into training, so no case separates a group-identity violation that respects temporal order from one that does not, and this paper explicitly does not know whether such a case would still be caught by the temporal check alone; a battery purpose-built to decouple the two conditions is the direct next step this paper's own evidence motivates. The frozen thresholds in Section 4.4, while genuinely fixed before evaluation, are nonetheless a single choice among many defensible ones; Section 5.5 already shows the extended check's own threshold is the specific reason Olist's group-overlap defect does not cross into BLOCKED under that approach specifically (though it does under the temporal check, which has no threshold to calibrate), so threshold sensitivity is not a minor caveat here but a result-shaping choice this paper reports transparently rather than tunes away. Both datasets concern late-delivery prediction specifically; whether this battery's findings generalise to other supply chain prediction tasks or to other governance tools is a question this design cannot answer. The two non-gate comparators, while intended to represent realistic non-specialist review practice, were authored by the same team that built the gate integration being compared against them. The label-availability correction (Section 4.2) addresses one specific, disclosed form of leakage in the reference population's own construction (an outcome not yet resolved by the training cutoff); it does not address every way a retrospective, delivered-only population (Olist) or a simulated dataset (DataCo) might still not represent the population a live system would actually score. Finally, this paper tests one version of one tool (BDP Model Gate v0.6.0) under one configuration; its findings are evidence about that tool's current default behaviour and this paper's specific extensions, not a general claim about automated governance tooling as a category.

# 8. Conclusion

Asked narrowly and testably — does this specific governance tool provide additional, actionable evidence for reviewing a late-delivery model before release, beyond what an AUROC-only, metrics-only, or lightweight automated-checklist review already provides — the answer this battery supports is a qualified yes, bounded to one defect type specifically, and on close inspection matched there by a trivial, near-free alternative this paper also tested. The stock BDP Model Gate reliably catches a schema mismatch no performance-only review can see at all, but a much cheaper automated checklist catches it identically; it reliably catches exact test-row duplication once enough of the true overlap survives whatever sample it is evaluated on, but the same cheap checklist, computing its overlap check on full data rather than a capped sample, catches it more reliably still; it misses a feature-combination leak under every condition this paper tested; and its own row-level comparison cannot see a genuine, distinct-record group-dependence overlap at any sample size, for a structural reason — but so does a one-line check of whether any training row's timestamp falls inside the scored window, at a fraction of the gate's cost, with a materially lower review burden, and with zero false positives across every held-out legitimate period this paper tested, where the stock and extended gate flagged nearly all of them. The practical guidance this paper offers a firm considering such a tool is correspondingly specific: trust its schema check once tested at your own evaluation scale; do not rely on its feature-level leakage check for a combination leak or its row-level overlap check for group-level dependence between structurally distinct records; never row-cap a check whose logic depends on comparing training against test rows without separately testing whether the cap preserves that check's stated sensitivity; add a one-line temporal-ordering check before building a more elaborate group-identity extension, since this evidence does not show the latter catching anything the former does not, in this battery; and track whether a flag on a legitimate case has independent, metrics-based corroboration before treating every BLOCKED or NEEDS_REVIEW verdict as equally informative, because, on the evidence reported here, they are not.
